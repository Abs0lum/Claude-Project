**Date:** 2026-10-03 · **Scale convention:** 1 block = 1 m; every metric figure below converts 1:1 to blocks. Imperial figures from sources are converted (1 ft = 0.3048 m) and rounded to the nearest block.

**How to read this document.** Section 1–4 are **SOURCED FACTS** — every numeric claim carries the URL it came from; where two sources disagree, both figures are given. Section 5 is **PROPOSED DESIGN** (mine): block programs for a *manor* (town / town II), a *city palace* (seat of government) and a *metropolis palace*, cut into ≤64 × 64 saved-structure pieces. Anything in Section 5 that is not explicitly tied to a source is a design proposal, not history. Where I could not source a detail I say so rather than invent a figure.

## 1. Real palaces and great houses — dimensions and plans

### 1.1 Versailles (France, 1660s–1780s)

**Overall.** The palace "covers about 63,154 square metres", has "2,300 rooms", "2,143 windows, 1,252 chimneys, and 67 staircases", a garden "façade of 402 metres", and is "covered by around 10 hectares" of roof ([Wikipedia – Palace of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Versailles)). The **Aile du Midi** (south wing) consists of "two parallel building bodies extending 150 metres north to south"; its **Galerie des Batailles** (a 19th-c. insertion) is 118.8 m long × 12.63 m wide × 13 m high, "the largest room in the château" ([fr.wikipedia – Aile du Midi](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aile_du_Midi)). The **Royal Chapel** is "40 metres high… 42 metres long and 24 metres wide" ([Wikipedia – Palace of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Versailles)).

**Hall of Mirrors.** "73 m long", "10.50 m deep", "12.30 m" high, "17 windows open in the direction of the park" facing "17 equally large mirrors… composed of more than 350 individual mirror surfaces", built 1678–1684 ([Wikipedia – Hall of Mirrors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_of_Mirrors)). Proportions: 7:1 length-to-width, and the room is taller than it is wide — the canonical "gallery" shape.

**The *appartement* as a sequence (King).** The *Appartement du roi* is entered from the Queen's Staircase and runs: (1) vestibule/loggia; (2) **Salle des Gardes** — the guard room, with "wooden benches, camp beds, and folding screens" for the bodyguard, where on Mondays a table was set at which "Louis XIV would personally accept petitions"; (3) **Première Antichambre / Salon du Grand Couvert** — the public dining room, lit by "three windows onto the cour de marbre… and three windows onto the cour de la reine"; (4) **Salon de l'Œil-de-Bœuf** — the main antechamber where "courtiers waited… before the king's lever"; (5) **Chambre du Roi** — the bedchamber (1701), with "an alcove with balustrade separating the bed area"; (6) **Cabinet du Conseil** — the council chamber, where "councils were held… private audiences most often occurred"; (7) **Cabinet des Perruques** — the wig/dressing cabinet, where the king changed "shirt, wig, and hat as many as four times a day" ([Wikipedia – Appartement du roi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appartement_du_roi)). The bedchamber is "centrally located on the étage noble and overlooks the cour de marbre"; "the central east-west axis of the palace, city, and garden designs bisects this room"; "Entry into the alcove behind the balustrade was strictly forbidden, unless authorized by the king" ([Wikipedia – Chambre du Roi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chambre_du_Roi)).

**The State Apartment (*Grand appartement du roi*)** — seven salons in enfilade, each with an evening function during the thrice-weekly *soirées d'appartement*: Diane (billiards), Mars (ballroom), Mercure (card gaming), Apollon (throne/music), Jupiter, Saturne, Vénus (buffet of food and drink) ([Wikipedia – Grand appartement du roi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_appartement_du_roi)). The **Salon d'Hercule** on the first floor "connects the Royal Chapel in the North Wing… with the grand appartement du roi" and under Louis XV "served as a ball room as the King felt the salon de Mars was too small and the Hall of Mirrors was too large" ([Wikipedia – Salon d'Hercule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_d%27Hercule)).

**The Queen's State Apartment** runs: Guard room (reached from the Queen's Staircase via a loggia) → **Antichambre du Grand Couvert** ("the King, Queen and other members of the royal family dined in public") → **Salon des Nobles** (formal audiences; otherwise "an antechamber to the Queen's bedroom") → **Chambre de la Reine** ([Wikipedia – Grand appartement de la reine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_appartement_de_la_reine)).

**The private suite behind the state rooms (*petits appartements*).** Louis XV's private apartment comprised the *Chambre de Louis XV* (1740), the *Salon des pendules*, the *Cabinet intérieur* ("served as a dining room; and… a workroom"), the *Arrière-cabinet* ("a private room (with a small cabinet de la chaise) that communicated directly with the degré du roi in which he conducted much of the day-to-day governance of France"), the *Salle à manger des retours de chasse* (1750), the *Pièce des buffets* (1754) and the private stair *degré du roi* ([Wikipedia – Petit appartement du roi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petit_appartement_du_roi)). Marie-Antoinette's private chambers occupy **two floors** "behind the Queen's State Apartment with windows overlooking modest inner courtyards": first floor — Méridienne room, Library (with "a concealed annexe"), Gold Room; second floor — billiard room, dining room/boudoir, **"Principal Chambermaids' Rooms: three chambers ranging from just over 8 m² to 12 m²"**, three further servants' rooms, two rooms for Count Fersen, and a lacquer room; three staircases link the floors ("the Billiard Staircase, the Fleury Staircase and the Dupes Staircase"); a hidden door "concealed in the wall hangings, behind the huge jewellery cabinet" leads out of the State Bedchamber ([Château de Versailles press kit, *Marie-Antoinette's Private Chambers*](https://en.chateauversailles.fr/sites/default/files/presse/documents/dp_cabinets_interieurs_eng_bd.pdf)).

**Service block — the Grand Commun.** "82 metres by 76", "15,000 m², spread over six floors plus the attic", finished 1684; "103 apartments" in 1684, "220 by 1722"; "nearly 1,500 people lived in the building, not counting the non-resident staff"; its "ground floor, remarkable for its six-metre-high vaulted ceiling, contains the kitchens, a chapel, dining rooms and offices"; it cooked for those "ayant bouche à cour" while the royal table was cooked inside the palace "for security reasons" ([Château de Versailles – The Grand Commun](https://en.chateauversailles.fr/discover/estate/palace/grand-commun)).

**Population.** "Depending on the day, there were between 3,000 and 10,000 people here"; "Members of the royal family and those with important roles had apartments overlooking the gardens", lesser courtiers "on the city-side or in the Palace outbuildings, such as the Grand Commun or the Stables"; posts were "inherited or could be bought" ([Château de Versailles – Courtiers](https://en.chateauversailles.fr/discover/history/key-dates/courtiers)). In 1722 "4,000 personnes logées dans l'enceinte même du château", c. 2,700 more in the dependencies, plus 1,434 royal guards; at the end of the old regime the château held "les appartements du roi et de la reine, de 20 appartements princiers et de 189 appartements à la destination des courtisans" ([fr.wikipedia – Château de Versailles](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau_de_Versailles)).

### 1.2 Hampton Court (England, 1515–1700s)

**Great Hall:** "106 ft. by 40 ft., and is 45 ft. high to the plate and 92 ft. to the top of the gable" → 32 × 12 blocks, 14 high to the wall-plate, 28 to the gable. **Great Gallery (Council Chamber):** "117 ft. long by 28 ft. high, and 24 ft. wide" → 36 × 7 × 8.5. **Fountain Court east elevation:** "300 ft. long and 60 ft. high, divided into twenty-three bays" → 91 m × 18 m ([British History Online – Hampton Court architectural description](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/vch/middx/vol2/pp371-379)). Floor area "47,330 m²", c. "1,000" rooms ([Wikipedia – List of largest palaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_palaces)).

**Access gradient.** Progression ran "Base Court (arrivals area)… the Great Hall for mid-ranking men, the Great Watching Chamber for higher ranks, then the Presence Chamber and Privy Chamber, finally reaching the monarch's private apartments"; "The guards at each successive doorway prevented the 'unworthy' from penetrating"; "as many as a thousand courtiers and their servants would cram into Hampton Court"; the **Lord Chamberlain's department** ran the state rooms and the **Lord Steward's department** the "'below stairs' areas… such as Henry's Kitchens"; "Thirty of the most pleasant guest suites… were in Base Court… with 'en suite' toilets and chimneys hidden neatly within the footprint" ([Historic Royal Palaces – The royal court in the Tudor period](https://www.hrp.org.uk/hampton-court-palace/history-and-stories/the-royal-court-in-the-tudor-period/)). A "rather insignificant doorway in the great watching chamber" led to the Presence Chamber with "guards in the royal Tudor livery keeping watch by the doorway"; "a spiral stone staircase connected Henry's bedchamber to the ground floor's Office of the King's Wardrobe"; a "bricked-up Tudor archway once connected the king's chambers directly to Cardinal Wolsey's adjacent lodgings" ([Tudor Travel Guide – Henry VIII's lost apartments](https://thetudortravelguide.com/henry-viiis-lost-apartments-at-hampton-court-palace/)).

**Kitchens.** "Built to feed over 450 people twice a day, the kitchens… were staffed by 200 people" ([HRP – Historic Cookery](https://www.hrp.org.uk/hampton-court-palace/whats-on/historic-cookery/)). A secondary source gives "55 rooms, covering 3,000 square feet", three Master Cooks (King, Queen, Court), departments each under a Sergeant with "yeoman, grooms, undercooks, scullions, and kitchen maids", "600-800 meals twice a day", "six massive fireplaces" in the Great Kitchen, rooms including "three larders (flesh, wet, and dry), dairy, pastry room, spicery, confectionery, boiling house, and pewter storage" ([Naked Kitchens – Henry VIII's 55-room kitchen](https://www.nakedkitchens.com/blog/henry-viiis-55-room-kitchen-at-hampton-court-palace)). *Caveat:* 55 rooms in 3,000 sq ft (279 m²) is implausible — 3,000 m² (≈32,000 sq ft) is the likelier reading; treat the area as unverified. Lower courtiers used the communal **Great House of Easement**: "an oak plank with holes cut at two-foot intervals", on two floors, draining by "a chute… into brick culverts which ran under the moat and into the river Thames"; "after the court had been here for four weeks, the brick chambers would fill head-high" ([Little Waldingfield History Society](https://littlewaldingfieldhistorysociety.wordpress.com/2015/12/18/hampton-court-palace-gardens-and-park-a-talk-by-dave-steward/the-great-house-of-easement/)).

**Tudor household scale.** In winter "between a thousand and fifteen hundred persons" of whom "only about a hundred had access to the King"; "only about a hundred courtiers were entitled to permanent rooms at court"; "Double lodgings had two rooms with fireplaces; single lodgings had one room"; a duke could bring twelve servants, the Lord Chamberlain ten, a Gentleman of the Privy Chamber four; the Sergeant Porter guarded the gates with "only five Yeomen and two Grooms" ([Weir, *Henry VIII: The King and His Court*, ch. 6 via erenow](https://erenow.org/biographies/henryviiithekingandhiscourt/6.php)). The household rule-book was the **Eltham Ordinance** of January 1526, drawn up by Wolsey ([Wikipedia – Eltham Ordinance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eltham_Ordinance)).

### 1.4 Doge's Palace, Venice (1340–1600s)

Footprint "approximately 71 m × 75 m"; the **Sala del Maggior Consiglio** is "54 meters long × 25 meters wide × 12 meters tall" ([Doge's Palace guide – map](https://dogespalace-guide.com/plan-your-visit/doges-palace-map/)) (elsewhere "53 m × 25 m", capacity "1,200–2,000 noblemen"; Senate "approximately 120 members"; Council of Ten "up to 17 members"; Doge's apartments "eight residential chambers"; New Prisons "~40 cells" — [Doge's Palace guide – rooms](https://dogespalace-guide.com/what-to-see/rooms-and-halls/)). Both main façades are "approximately 75 meters" ([Doge's Palace guide – façade](https://dogespalace-guide.com/what-to-see/facade-and-courtyard/)). Vertical zoning: **ground floor** offices and prisons ("more prison spaces were created to occupy the entire ground floor of the southern wing"), **first floor** the Doge's "prestigious, though not particularly large, residence", **second floor** the institutional chambers (Senate, Council of Ten, Great Council); prisons in three tiers — *Pozzi* ("dark, damp and isolated" ground-floor wells), *Piombi* (upper east wing, 1591, "directly under the lead roof"), and the New Prisons across the canal via the Bridge of Sighs (1614), which "contains two separate corridors that run next to each other" ([Wikipedia – Doge's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doge%27s_Palace)).

### 1.5 Palazzo Vecchio and Palazzo Pitti, Florence

**Palazzo Vecchio** (begun 1299): tower "95 meters"; **Salone dei Cinquecento** 54 × 23 × 18 m, begun 1494 for Savonarola's Grand Council of 500; houses the Studiolo, the Quartiere degli Elementi, the Sala dei Gigli and the Cancelleria; the tower held the cell nicknamed the "*Albergaccio*" where Savonarola was imprisoned ([Visit Florence – Palazzo Vecchio](https://www.visitflorence.com/florence-monuments/palazzo-vecchio.html); dimensions also at [Tickets Florence – Hall of the Five Hundred](https://www.tickets-florence.it/palazzo-vecchio/hall-of-the-five-hundred/)). **Palazzo Pitti**: corps de logis "32,000 square metres"; "140 rooms open to the public"; Royal Apartments 14 rooms, Palatine Gallery 28 ([Wikipedia – Palazzo Pitti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palazzo_Pitti)); façade "250 meters in length" per [nss magazine](https://www.nssmag.com/en/pills/17295/5-facts-to-know-about-palazzo-pitti) (other published figures of c. 205 m exist but were not verified here). The **Vasari Corridor** (1565, built in five months for a Medici wedding) is "approximately one kilometer" long and let the Duke "move undisturbed between both palaces", running from the Palazzo Vecchio through the Uffizi, over the Ponte Vecchio and the church of Santa Felicità to the Pitti ([Wikipedia – Vasari Corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasari_Corridor)).

### 1.6 Topkapı, Istanbul (1460s–1850s)

Total area "from around 592,600 m²… to 700,000 m²"; four courtyards of increasing restriction — the second (Divan Square) "surrounded by the former palace hospital, bakery, Janissary quarters, stables, the imperial harem and Divan"; the **Harem** "contained more than 400 rooms"; the Imperial Council met in the domed *Kubbealtı*, which the sultan watched "from a window with a golden grill"; kitchens "form two rows of 20 wide chimneys" with "more than 800 people" cooking for "about 4,000 people" ([Wikipedia – Topkapı Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkap%C4%B1_Palace)). Another guide gives "around 5,000 palace staff every day… could reach 15,000" on holidays and a harem of "around 300 rooms, nine hammams, and two mosques" ([The Guide Istanbul – Topkapı](https://www.theguideistanbul.com/topkapi-palace/)). Harem population estimates range from 49 (1574) to 1,100–1,200 (1604–07) and 720 (Selim III); the eunuch corps "numbered between 800 and 1,200" c. 1600 ([Wikipedia – Ottoman Imperial Harem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Imperial_Harem)).

### 1.7 Forbidden City, Beijing (1406–1420)

"961 meters long from north to south, 753 meters wide", "about 720,000 square meters", wall circumference "over 3,400 meters", "9,371 rooms" today; **Hall of Supreme Harmony** "2,377 square meters", "35.05 meters" high; Gate of Supreme Harmony "1,300 square meters" ([Travel China Guide – How big is the Forbidden City](https://www.travelchinaguide.com/how-big-is-forbidden-city.htm)). The hall is "65 by 37 metres", "eleven bays wide… and five bays deep", rising "some 30 metres above the level of the surrounding square" ([Wikipedia – Hall of Supreme Harmony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_of_Supreme_Harmony)). Its forecourt is "30,000 square meters" and "accommodated up to 100,000 people"; the Palace of Heavenly Purity is "24 meters high"; the imperial garden "12,000 square meters" ([Facts and Details – Forbidden City](https://factsanddetails.com/china/cat15/sub94/entry-6465.html)). The moat is "as wide as 52 meters"; the complex holds "more than 90 palace compounds"; consorts lived in "six identical, walled palace compounds" on each side of the Inner Court ([Smarthistory – The Forbidden City](https://smarthistory.org/the-forbidden-city/)).

### 1.8 Medieval royal palaces: Westminster and the Palais de la Cité

**Westminster Hall** (1097–99; roof 1393–1401): "20.7 by 73.2 metres (68 by 240 feet)", roof timber "some 660 tons", lead "about 176 tons", wooden arches spanning "18 metres (60 feet) or more" ([UK Parliament – hammer-beam roof](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/building/palace/westminsterhall/architecture/the-hammer-beam-roof-/)); floor "about 1,547 sq m"; it housed the Courts of King's Bench, Common Pleas and (from the 15th c.) Chancery, coronation banquets, and "merchant shops and stalls… removed in 1780" ([Wikipedia – Westminster Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Hall)). "The Court of Exchequer… was in a building adjoining the North of the West wall"; the hall had "an arcade with large arches and windows and a wall passage around all four sides"; the White Hall stood south of the Great Hall, the Painted Chamber, Queen's Chamber and Queen's Chapel "to the South East of the site, with access to the Garden and the River" ([Sarah Woodbury – Westminster Palace](https://www.sarahwoodbury.com/westminster-palace/)). The **Painted Chamber** — "82 feet (25.0 m)" × "28 feet (8.5 m)" — was the king's bedchamber *and* state reception room, later the Lords' meeting place ([Wikipedia – Painted Chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painted_Chamber)). The 1834 fire began with "an overheated stove used to destroy the Exchequer's stockpile of tally sticks" ([Wikipedia – History of the Palace of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Palace_of_Westminster)). The modern palace has "over 1,100 rooms, 100 staircases and 3 miles (4.8 km) of passageways", floor area "112,476 m²" ([Wikipedia – Palace of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster)).

**Palais de la Cité, Paris.** The **Salle des Gens d'Armes** (the household's dining/guard hall, under the Grand'Salle) is "63.3 meters long, 27.4 meters wide, and 8.5 meters high" and "could serve as many as two thousand persons"; the **Salle des Gardes** "22.8 meters long, 11.8 meters wide, and 6.9 meters high"; the palace held the *Chambre des Comptes* ("the treasury of the kingdom"), the Parlement's *Grand'Chambre* and *Chambre des Enquêtes* (high courts), and the royal archives; towers held "prison cells and torture chambers" ([Wikipedia – Palais de la Cité](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palais_de_la_Cit%C3%A9)). The Conciergerie article gives the Gens d'Armes as "originally 68 m… now 61.2 m… 28 m wide, and 8.7 m high" with "four large fireplaces", kitchens of c. 1353 on two levels, and two prisoner classes — *pailleux* on straw in collective cells and *pistoliers* who bribed for beds ([Wikipedia – Conciergerie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conciergerie)).

### 1.9 Bishops' palaces

**Wells** (c. 1210–1292, walled 14th c.): chapel and Great Hall "built between 1275 and 1292 for Bishop Robert Burnell"; the hall was a "five-bayed aisled hall", the palace "a two-storey building of seven bays", with a "three-storey gatehouse… from 1341", "5 metres (16 ft) crenellated walls" and a moat ([Wikipedia – Bishop's Palace, Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop%27s_Palace,_Wells)); walls "average 5 m high" with "6 bastion towers" ([Gatehouse Gazetteer – Wells](https://www.gatehouse-gazetteer.info/English%20sites/3286.html)). **Lincoln**: East Hall "built over an undercroft… completed in the 1230s"; chapel range and tower gatehouse 1430s ([Wikipedia – Lincoln Medieval Bishop's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Medieval_Bishop%27s_Palace)). No hall dimensions were found for either; the generic medieval hall rule applies (§1.11).

### 1.11 Rules of thumb extracted from the sourced data

- **Great hall proportion:** "a rectangular room between one and a half and three times as long as it was wide, and also higher than it was wide"; dais at the upper end, screens passage at the lower end with "two openings" to buttery and pantry, kitchen beyond, minstrels' gallery over the screens; the household "would dine together" and some "might sleep on the floor" ([Wikipedia – Great hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_hall)). Sourced examples: Westminster 73 × 21 (3.5:1), Hampton Court 32 × 12 × 14 (to plate), Gens d'Armes 63 × 27 × 8.5, Doge's Great Council 54 × 25 × 12, Salone dei Cinquecento 54 × 23 × 18, Kedleston 20 × 11 × 12.
- **Gallery proportion:** 7:1 to 9:1, taller than wide (Hall of Mirrors 73 × 10.5 × 12.3; Galerie des Batailles 119 × 12.6 × 13; Hampton Court Great Gallery 36 × 7 × 8.5; Harewood 23 × 7 × 6.4).
- **Storey heights:** state rooms 8–13 m (Gens d'Armes 8.5; Great Gallery 8.5; Hall of Mirrors 12.3); service vaults 6 m (Grand Commun); a mansion kitchen "never… less than 10 feet high… 20 feet will not be too much in the largest" ([Kerr 1865, archive.org text](https://archive.org/details/gentlemanshouse01kerrgoog)); maids' rooms 8–12 m² (Versailles).
- **Vertical zoning:** offices and cells on the ground floor, the ruler's residence on the first, the great councils on the second (Doge's Palace); or state floor on the first (*étage noble*) with lodgings in attics and mezzanines (Versailles — courtiers lodged on "four floors… includ[ing] the attic apartments and the mezzanines", [This is Versailles – Courtiers' Lodgings](http://thisisversaillesmadame.blogspot.com/2019/11/the-courtiers-lodgings.html)).

## 2. The hidden world: jib doors, secret passages, servants' corridors and the below-stairs machine

### 2.1 Jib doors and the "invisible" door

A **jib door** is "a door made flush with a wall without dressings or moldings and often disguised by continuing the finishings or decorations of the wall across its surface" ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/jib%20door)). In Georgian/Regency houses they served "aesthetic design: maintaining architectural symmetry" and "servant concealment: disguising servant access points so household help remained 'invisible'", camouflaged by paint, wallpaper "with patterns aligned across the door", mouldings and skirtings, or "in libraries, fitted bookshelves or trompe-l'œil painted bookcases" (Killerton's library door carries joke titles such as *"Squeak on Openings"* and *"Hinge's Orations"*); "jib doors remained detectable upon close inspection due to visible seams" ([Regency Redingote – Jib-Doors Through the Regency](https://regencyredingote.wordpress.com/2019/05/03/jib-doors-through-the-regency/)).

### 2.2 Secret passages — the documented cases

- **Versailles, 6 October 1789.** The queen's bedchamber door is "disguised as a section of carved wooden panelling, flush with the wall"; the palace contains "dozens of these invisible doors" because "servants needed to move through Versailles without crossing royal spaces" and "courtiers needed access to private staircases"; Louis XVI kept an iron safe "behind a wooden panel near [his] private apartments" ([In Love With France – Secret rooms at Versailles](https://inlovewithfrance.com/952/the-secret-rooms-at-versailles-that-most-visitors-never-find/)). The door sits left of the bed and leads through her private rooms "toward the Oeil-de-Boeuf and the King's chambers"; at "about half-past four in the morning" the mob broke in and a bodyguard at the other door was "struck… his face… covered with blood" as the queen fled through it ([Important to Madeleine – The Door](https://importanttomadeleine.blogspot.com/2014/07/the-door.html)). The passage "originated during Maria Theresa's time" as "a suite of service rooms" that doubled as "a private means by which the king and queen could communicate"; its entrance is "a door located on the west side of the north wall of the chambre de la reine" ([Wikipedia – Petit appartement de la reine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petit_appartement_de_la_reine)).
- **Doge's Palace "Secret Itineraries".** The route climbs from the *Pozzi* through the offices of the Ducal Notary and the *Deputato alla Segreta*, the Great Chancellor's office ("a central archive") and the **Secret Chancellery** with "decorated cabinets that once stored public records and the secret documents", to the Torture Chamber, the Inquisitors' chamber (1539) and the **Chamber of the Three Head Magistrates**, "with a secret passage to the Council Chamber" ([Palazzo Ducale – Special itineraries](https://palazzoducale.visitmuve.it/en/layout-and-collections/special-itineraries/)). The Inquisitors' room has "a secret entrance behind a wooden wardrobe" to the Council of Ten; the Chancellery lockers carry coats-of-arms "dating from 1268 onward"; the attic over the Great Council hall "housed weapons" ([Justin Plus Lauren – Secret Itineraries](https://justinpluslauren.com/doges-palace-secret-itineraries-tour-venice/)). The *Piombi* were "cramped attic cells beneath the palace roof… named after the lead sheets", from which Casanova escaped in 1756 ([Walks of Italy – Inside Doge's Palace](https://www.walksofitaly.com/blog/attractions/inside-doges-palace)).
- **Palazzo Vecchio.** In 1342 the short-lived ruler (Walter of Brienne, "Duke of Athens") "had a small door installed in the alleyway, with a narrow staircase leading to the upper floors" so that "the ruler himself, spies and special visitors — could enter and leave without being seen"; Cosimo I's **Tesoretto** had "hidden compartments for storing precious objects and important papers and also two hidden doorways" behind the wall paintings, with windows to watch the Uffizi and the courtyard; Francesco I's **Studiolo** likewise had "compartments and doorways hidden behind paintings"; the Salone's ceiling was raised "several meters" and a hidden staircase reaches the truss attic above it ([Our 10 Year Plan – Palazzo Vecchio Secret Passages](http://our10yearplan.blogspot.com/2017/04/museums-palazzo-vecchio-secret-passages.html)). The Studiolo was "designed in the 1570s" and the Tesoretto was "Cosimo I's treasury" ([Tickets Florence](https://www.tickets-florence.it/palazzo-vecchio/hall-of-the-five-hundred/)). The Vasari Corridor (§1.5) is the same idea at city scale.
- **Hampton Court.** A spiral stair from Henry VIII's bedchamber to the Wardrobe office below, and a now bricked-up arch to Wolsey's lodgings ([Tudor Travel Guide](https://thetudortravelguide.com/henry-viiis-lost-apartments-at-hampton-court-palace/)); the Wolsey Closet is "a small Tudor room… likely a room for private conversations"; a Tudor stair in the Horn Room is "worn down by decades worth of scurrying servants" ([Living London History – Ten secrets](https://livinglondonhistory.com/ten-secrets-of-hampton-court-palace/)).

### 2.3 The service corridor, the back stairs and the green baize door

Kerr's governing principle (1865): "let the servants have access to all their duties without coming unexpectedly upon the family or visitors… The family constitute one community: the servants another… each class is entitled to shut its door upon the other and be alone" ([Kerr, *The Gentleman's House*, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gentlemanshouse01kerrgoog)). Mechanisms: "Small staircases led to convenient points… allowing servants to enter reception rooms when required, without being seen"; "Long and complicated passages linking kitchens with dining rooms were devised; in some houses the tortuous route… could be an eighth of a mile" (≈200 m); "The single door was lined with green baize to deaden any sound"; "In the grander houses only male servants were allowed to be visible, and then only when required" ([Wikipedia – Servants' quarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servants%27_quarters)). The baize door — "an ordinary framed door with sturdy green cloth attached using brass tacks" — sat at "the dividing line between the two domains", absorbing noise and kitchen smells; housemaids "were taught to turn their face to the wall if they should pass their employer on the stairs" ([Jane Austen's World – The Green Baize Door](https://janeaustensworld.com/2012/01/27/the-green-baize-door-dividing-line-between-servant-and-master/)). Kerr on the **dinner route**: "as the dishes must be carried to and from the door through the family part of the house… it is essential that they shall not cross the track of family traffic"; a "delivery-hatch… (like the 'buttery hatch' of the mediæval time), opening from the Kitchen to a Corridor"; "The passage-way from the Kitchen to the Main House ought of course to be wide throughout, and thoroughly ventilated; and no Staircase ought to open out of it to carry the odours upwards"; a basement kitchen needs "a special Dinner-Stair… or… a Lift" ([Kerr, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gentlemanshouse01kerrgoog)). (No source gave an explicit *width* for service corridors; the 2-block width in §5 is my proposal.)

### 2.4 The domestic offices and their sizes (Kerr, 1865)

- **Kitchen:** small house "15 to 18 feet square"; mansion "18 or 20 feet by 25 or 30" (6 × 8–9 blocks), "never… less than 10 feet high… 20 feet… in the largest"; "in no circumstances ought a Kitchen to include the fittings proper to a Scullery" ([Kerr, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/gentlemanshouse01kerrgoog)).
- **Servants' Hall:** position "first near the Kitchen… secondly between the Kitchen and the Butler's-Pantry… Fifthly… near the Back-door… as it is the waiting-room for all persons of the rank of the under servants"; upper servants eat separately "in the Steward's-room and Housekeeper's-room"; large houses add a "Women's Work-room" ([Kerr](https://archive.org/details/gentlemanshouse01kerrgoog)); at table "the butler and housekeeper presided… much as the master and lady of the house did 'above stairs'" ([Wikipedia – Servants' hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servants%27_hall)).
- **Butler's Pantry:** "from 12 or 14 feet square up to twice that size", near the Dining-room but away from the back door "for security of plate"; **Serving-room** "10 to 20 feet square"; **Wash-house** c. 20–25 × 15–20 ft; **Gun-room** "twelve to fifteen feet square" ([Susanna Ives – Domestic Offices c. 1865](https://susannaives.com/wordpress/2012/09/domestic-offices-in-english-homes-circa-1865/)). The pantry "often adjoined the butler's bedroom for security"; the **Still Room** had "an oven, hot-plate, sink, and dresser, used for preparing tea, coffee, preserves, and pastries"; the **Housekeeper's Room** was "the primary business center… near the kitchen, servants' hall, or servants' entrance", also holding "preserves, linens, and household supplies"; **Store Rooms** "dry, cool, well-ventilated" ([Geri Walton – Upper-servant offices](https://www.geriwalton.com/what-are-upper-servant-offices/)). A house "would… have a butler's pantry with a specifically constructed repository for the valuable plate"; "The maids often slept two to a room in the attic"; men's quarters "separate from the women's" ([Regina Jeffers – Housing the Servants](https://reginajeffers.blog/2024/09/27/life-below-stairs-part-5-housing-the-servants/)).
- **Where servants slept:** "attics (cold in winter, hot in summer), brick wings behind kitchens, and basements… gardeners in bothies to monitor greenhouses, grooms over stables, footmen near silver closets for security" ([British Heritage – Servants' lives](https://britishheritage.com/history/servants-lives-below-stairs)). Kerr: female domestics "on the uppermost story, or over the Offices, accessible by the Back-Staircase… of small size, suitable for not more than two persons… Every room ought to have a fireplace", menservants "in a separate quarter… each man… a separate room", ladies' maids on the family's bedroom floor "at hand" ([Kerr](https://archive.org/details/gentlemanshouse01kerrgoog)). The scullery floor "was lower than the kitchen's, which prevented water from flowing into the cooking areas" ([Jane Austen's World – Servants' quarters](https://janeaustensworld.com/2011/01/29/the-servants-quarters-in-19th-century-country-houses-like-downton-abbey/)).

### 2.5 Bells

"During the 18th century, the only way of summoning a servant was by calling, or a handbell"; the "early 19th century invention of the bell pull" changed this ([Wikipedia – Servants' quarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servants%27_quarters)). Erddig "displays rows of brass bells summoning servants… 'no such thing… as being off duty'"; housemaids rose at 5:30 a.m.; one day off a month ([British Heritage](https://britishheritage.com/history/servants-lives-below-stairs)). Bells hung in the servants' hall ([Jane Austen's World](https://janeaustensworld.com/2011/01/29/the-servants-quarters-in-19th-century-country-houses-like-downton-abbey/)).

## 3. Public administration inside a palace or town hall

**Exchequer (treasury + audit).** Named for its table — "10 feet (3.0 m) by 5 feet (1.5 m)" with "a black cloth bearing stripes of about the breadth of a human hand in a chequer-pattern" on which counters were moved. Two divisions: the **Lower Exchequer / Exchequer of Receipt**, which "received deposits of money from revenue collectors and issued wooden tally sticks as receipts" and was "the physical treasury", and the **Upper Exchequer**, which "functioned as a Court of Audit" where "Barons of the Exchequer would sit at the counting table" to examine the sheriffs' accounts; officers: Treasurer, Chancellor of the Exchequer, Barons, Chamberlains, King's Remembrancer, with the Chief Justiciar at the head of the table; oldest Pipe Roll 1130; the *Dialogus de Scaccario* describing it dates "from the late 1170s" ([Wikipedia – Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchequer)). At Westminster it occupied "a building adjoining the North of the West wall" of the hall (§1.8). Town-hall treasuries: Bruges' tower (charters, seals, coffers), Kraków's 36 m² tower room "for storing the treasury and insignia", Ypres' tower, Nottingham's "strong iron chest" in the council chamber (§1.10).

**Chancery (writing office, seals, archives).** "A medieval writing office, responsible for the production of official documents", headed by the chancellor, issuing "all the charters and writs, which were all sealed with the Great Seal"; enrolled on Charter Rolls (from 1199), Patent Rolls (1201), Close Rolls (1204); "began as part of the royal household, but by the 13th century was separate… located at Westminster" ([Wikipedia – Chancery (medieval office)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancery_(medieval_office))). London had "approximately 120 Chancery clerks" in the 14th–15th c.: twelve **Clerks of the First Form** (Keeper of the Rolls, two Preceptors, six Examiners, a Notary), twelve **Clerks of the Second Form** (Clerks of the Crown, Clerk of the Petty Bag, Under-Clerk of Parliament) and **cursitors and under-clerks** who did the writing; headquartered at the Domus Conversorum on Chancery Lane, the clerks "lodged in nearby inns"; they lived on fees and property dealing ([Medieval Londoners – Chancery Clerks](https://medievallondoners.ace.fordham.edu/chancery-clerks/)). Venice's equivalent — Great Chancellor's office as "central archive" and the Secret Chancellery's cabinets — is in §2.2; Kraków's chancery kept "city books" upstairs (§1.10).

**Council chamber and courts.** Councils met in the king's *Cabinet du Conseil* (Versailles), the Council of Ten's chamber (17 members) and Senate (~120) on the Doge's second floor, Siena's Sala dei Nove and Sala del Mappamondo, Brussels' U-shaped benches; courts sat in the great hall itself (Westminster's three courts; Brussels' Gothic Room "courtroom"; Wrocław's Court Chamber where hearings were "previously held under the open sky"; Oxford's hall "divided into two court rooms by rows of pillars") — all §1.8–1.10. Petitions were received by Louis XIV in the **guard room** on Mondays (§1.1).

**Guard rooms and prisons.** Guard rooms sit at the apartment entrance (Versailles' *Salle des Gardes* with "camp beds"; the Palais de la Cité's 23 × 12 × 7 m *Salle des Gardes*). Prisons: Doge's Pozzi/Piombi/New Prisons (~40 cells); Palazzo Vecchio's tower cell; Ypres' and Kraków's towers; Nottingham's rock-cut dungeons (7 × 4 × 2 m); Oxford's Bocardo over the North Gate with "the freemen's ward above the gate… an inner ward… and below the wards two begging-rooms"; the Conciergerie's *pailleux*/*pistole* classes (§1.8–1.10). Prisons were fee-based businesses (gaoler's fees "debtors, felons, 14s. 8d.", [Nottinghamshire History](http://www.nottshistory.org.uk/books/potterbriscoe1895/guildhallp2.htm)).

**Officers as symbolic villager jobs.**
- **Treasurer / Chamberlain of the Exchequer** — receives coin, cuts tallies, audits at the chequered table ([Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchequer)).
- **Chancery clerk / cursitor** — writes writs and charters, enrols them, keeps the seal ([Medieval Londoners](https://medievallondoners.ace.fordham.edu/chancery-clerks/)).
- **Reeve** — "the pivot man of the manorial system", chosen "annually at Michaelmas", often "elected by the peasants", who "oversee[s] the work which the peasants were bound to perform" and handles "the sale of produce, collection of monies and payment of accounts" ([Wikipedia – Reeve (England)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reeve_(England))).
- **Bailiff** — "the senior person living on the manor if the lord was absent", "chief law officer and business manager", who "was supposed to view the whole manor every day", collects rents, buys "salt and iron"; "usually hated by the tenants" ([April Munday – Medieval bailiff](https://aprilmunday.wordpress.com/tag/medieval-bailiff/)).
- **Steward** — manages several manors, "rarely handled daily operations" ([Reeve (England)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reeve_(England))); castle steward "looked after the entire household" ([Medieval Chronicles](https://www.medievalchronicles.com/medieval-castles/castles-jobs-staff-and-roles/)).
- **Constable** — of a fortress: custody of "the fortress itself", "ultimately responsible for the prisoners", "building maintenance, soldiers' pay", answerable "body for body" for prisoners ([Wikipedia – Constable of the Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constable_of_the_Tower)); of a castle household: "charged with horses, grooms and page" ([Medieval Chronicles](https://www.medievalchronicles.com/medieval-castles/castles-jobs-staff-and-roles/)).
- **Sheriff (shire-reeve)** — royal officer who "enforced legislation and royal decrees" and "presided over local courts"; **portreeve** — officer of a trading town ([Reeve (England)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reeve_(England))).
- **Porter / gate guard** — "questioned visitors, inspected carts, and monitored the comings and goings of servants, merchants, and travelers"; captain of the guard above them ([Castles and History – Guards and watchmen](https://castlesandhistory.com/medieval-castle-guards-and-watchmen-the-vigilance-behind-the-walls/)).
- **Herald** — *not separately sourced in this pass* (the reference page could not be fetched); treat the herald as a symbolic proclamation/messenger role until a source is added.

## 4. Apartments as old palaces had them

**Versailles.** Late-regime count: 20 princely apartments and **189 courtiers' apartments** in the château, plus 220 in the Grand Commun (§1.1). "Besides the royal family, certain aristocrats were entitled to a lodging at court… those who were occupying key positions in the royal households"; apartments "were constantly changing. Depending on status, employment and royal favour courtiers were moved from one apartment to the next"; courtiers were lodged on "four floors… includ[ing] the attic apartments and the mezzanines" ([This is Versailles – Courtiers' Lodgings](http://thisisversaillesmadame.blogspot.com/2019/11/the-courtiers-lodgings.html)). Documented examples: the Duc d'Aumont (First Gentleman of the Bedchamber) had "just three rooms… not much compared to the eight rooms assigned to the First Maître d'Hôtel", "two fireplaces [but] no place to reheat food"; the Duc de Bouillon's 1755 apartment near the chapel had "11 separate rooms: wardrobe, passage, toilet, cabinet, bedchamber, first antechamber, second antechamber, grand cabinet, inner cabinet and toilet"; the Comtesse de Narbonne had 13 rooms including rooms for "maid-servant" and "servant"; the Comte de Béthune had five rooms in the Ministers' Wing with "fireplaces in four out of five rooms" and "a stove for reheating meals" ([Courtiers' Lodgings I](http://thisisversaillesmadame.blogspot.com/2019/11/the-courtiers-lodgings-i.html)); the Bishop of Laon (Grand Almoner) was lodged "in the attic of the Corps Central… right above the Salon of the Grand Covert… Three large chambers and several smaller cabinets" ([Courtiers' Lodgings III](http://thisisversaillesmadame.blogspot.com/2019/11/the-courtiers-lodgings-iii.html)). **Pattern:** a lodging = antechamber + bedchamber + cabinet (+ wardrobe/garde-robe), 3–13 rooms, allocated by office; servants of the lodger squeezed into a closet or the entresol.

**Hampton Court grace-and-favour.** From the 1730s; "53 original apartments and detached houses… rose to almost 70 at its height"; up to "100 residents… employing 200 to 300 domestic servants"; "an average of 14 rooms, often of vastly differing sizes arranged in a haphazard configuration, sometimes over several floors"; granted "rent-free… to those who had given great service to crown or country, or… their widows" (only 4% to men); under Victoria "a waiting list of up to 20 years"; many "had no bathroom or central heating"; last grants in the 1960s ([Historia – Grace and Favour at Hampton Court](https://historiamag.com/grace-favour-hampton-court/)). Tudor lodgings: ~100 permanent rooms; double lodgings of two rooms with fireplaces (§1.2).

**Forbidden City.** The emperor's consorts occupied twelve compounds — "six identical, walled palace compounds" either side ([Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/the-forbidden-city/)); "Each palace is a separated courtyard which has a front hall, a rear hall and assistant halls" ([Beijing Trip – Six Western Palaces](https://www.beijingtrip.com/attractions/forbidden/palaces.htm)), i.e. "a courtyard, where guests were received, a main hall with a one bedroom and one side hall"; a (likely inflated) Ming claim puts "9,000 imperial concubines and imperial palace maids" in residence ([Facts and Details](https://factsanddetails.com/china/cat15/sub94/entry-6465.html)). The Topkapı harem (300–400+ rooms) is the Ottoman equivalent (§1.6).

**Doge's Palace.** The Doge's own lodging was only "eight residential chambers" on the first floor ([Doge's Palace guide](https://dogespalace-guide.com/what-to-see/rooms-and-halls/)) — a reminder that in a *republican* seat the ruler's apartment is small and the councils are large.

## 5. PROPOSED CIVITAS PALACE PROGRAMS (design, not history)

### 5.1 Design rules derived from §1–4

1. **Three vertical zones** (Doge's model + Versailles): ground = offices, guard rooms, kitchens, cells (height 5); *étage noble* = state rooms (height 8; great hall and gallery rise to 12); attic/mezzanine = lodgings and servants (height 3–4).
2. **The *appartement* grammar** (Versailles): guard room → antechamber (public dining) → grand antechamber (waiting) → bed/audience chamber → council/cabinet → garde-robe. Access filters at each door (Hampton Court). Every bedchamber has an alcove with a 1-high balustrade (fence) and a **jib door** in the alcove wall.
3. **Hidden spine corridor, 2 blocks wide × 3 high**, between the state rooms and either the outer wall or the gallery's inner wall, with a jib door into every state room, back stairs (3 × 3 spiral) at each end and at mid-length, and a bell line (redstone) running inside it. Width is my choice (one block is walkable but blocks two-way traffic; two allows passing).
4. **Kitchen distance:** dining room ≤ 40 blocks by corridor from the kitchen hatch, never through family space (Kerr); kitchen floor 5–6 high with hood/chimney (Grand Commun's 6 m vaults, Kerr's 10–20 ft).
5. **Baize door** at the one junction between family and service zones — in-game a *warped door* (teal-green) framed in green wool.
6. **Lodgings** = antechamber + bedchamber + cabinet (3 rooms) as the standard; senior = 5 rooms; garrets 3 × 4 for servants (Versailles maids' rooms 8–12 m²).
7. **Bedrock job mechanics constrain "stations":** job-site blocks are detected only within "a 16 block radius and 4 block height" of the villager ([Minecraft Wiki – Villager](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Villager)), so a worker's bed must be on the same floor (or ±4 blocks) and within 16 blocks of its job block. Attic servants cannot "find" ground-floor kitchen stations — so put working servants' beds on the ground/mezzanine floor near their stations and reserve the attics for symbolic lodgers, or let the CIVITAS economy script assign roles directly.

### 5.6 Piece plan (how the 64 × 64 saved structures tile)

- **Manor:** 1 piece (56 × 44 inside a 64 × 64 save).
- **City palace:** 2 × 2 grid. NW = W half of corps + W wing + NW service court (kitchen block); NE = E half of corps + E wing + NE service court (stables/laundry); SW/SE = gate range halves + court paving; seams fall at x = 64 (through the great hall — use a glazed partition line as the seam marker) and z = 64 (through the court). Save with consistent origin corners and rotate none.
- **Metropolis:** 4 × 3 grid of 64 × 64 (A1–D3). Row 1 (north): A1 = NW garden pavilion + W end of corps (grand stair), B1–C1 = corps centre (gallery spans B1–C1, seam at x = 128 — put the seam on a mirror pier, not a window), D1 = E end of corps + chapel link. Rows 2–3: A2–A3 = N wing (chapel, theatre), D2–D3 = S wing (consort's apartment then courtiers), B2–C3 = court + Marble Court; the ministers' wings and forecourt sit in a fourth row (B4–C4) if the site allows, else as separate 48 × 14 saves. Annexes: Commons (1), Stables (1), Guard & Prison (1).

### 5.7 Which spaces drive the simulation vs. which are theatre

- **Stations (real job blocks, Bedrock-detectable):** chancery lecterns (librarians), land-registry cartography tables (cartographers), kitchens (smokers = butchers; composters = farmers for the kitchen garden), still room/sacristy (brewing stands = clerics), laundry (cauldrons = leatherworkers), smithy (smithing table = toolsmith), guard room/armoury (blast furnace = armorer, grindstone = weaponsmith), larder (barrel = fisherman). Place each station ≤ 16 blocks horizontally and ≤ 4 vertically from its worker's bed ([Minecraft Wiki – Villager](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Villager)).
- **Symbolic (no vanilla job block; flavour for CIVITAS' economy script):** treasurer/exchequer, judges and court rooms, council chamber, heralds, porters and gate guards, stables, prison, chapel nave, gallery, state apartments, baize door, bells.
- **Economic-movement hooks (for the script, not vanilla):** the public offices range on the street is where "tax", "writ" and "licence" transactions can be staged; the kitchen hatch and the Commons' dining halls are the food-distribution nodes ("bouche à cour"); the stables and smithy are the transport/repair node.

### 5.8 Verification steps before committing pieces

1. Build one 19 × 7 × 8 apartment unit and one 2 × 3 spine-corridor segment with a jib door and a painting door; confirm a villager paths through both and that a bell wired from a button in the room rings in a servants' hall 20 blocks away.
2. Confirm the 16-block / 4-block job-site rule on PS5 by placing a lectern on the 1st floor and a bed in the attic (9 blocks up): the villager should **not** claim it; move the bed to the mezzanine (≤ 4 up) and it should.
3. Save a 64 × 64 piece containing the 12-high gallery section and check the seam alignment (mirror pier at x = 128) after loading two pieces side by side.
