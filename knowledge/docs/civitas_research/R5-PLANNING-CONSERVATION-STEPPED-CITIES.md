## 1. Medieval → Renaissance city planning

### 1.1 Planned towns: bastides, Zähringer towns, Florentine terre nuove, Winchelsea

- Almost 700 bastides were founded in south-west France between 1222 (Cordes-sur-Ciel) and 1372; main streets "usually 6–10 m", alleys "5–6 m" or only "2–2.5 m"; a common house lot "8 m by 24 m"; squares "50 m to 55 m on each side" (a closed example 70 × 72 m); lots per town ran from ten to several thousand (3,000 at Grenade-sur-Garonne); each foundation had a charter of franchises and customs, and lords backed them "to generate revenues from taxes on trade rather than tithes" [1].
- Monpazier (1284, Edward I with Pierre de Gontaut; planner Bertrand de Panissals): plateau "400 by 220 metres", principal streets 8 m, 2,000–2,500 people at its height, six gates (2 N, 2 S, 1 E, 1 W), settlers given free wood and stone for a year and required to build within three; Thursday market [2].
- Grenade-sur-Garonne: charter lots of 5 × 15 *brasses* (1.835 m → ≈9.2 × 27.5 m); measured main streets ≈8.55 m (intended 9.175 m), side streets ≈7.02 m (7.34 m); blocks 55.05 / 82.44 / 110.11 m (1 : 1.5 : 2); 3,000 lots planned, ≈800 occupied by the 15th century; the church took twelve lots; each household also had a quarter-arpent garden and a one-arpent field [3]. Named planners and surveyors include Henry le Waleys (New Winchelsea 1281), the *capitano* who laid out Scarperia (1306) and Master James of St George (Welsh towns 1278–98), working with measuring rope and rods [4].
- Bern (after 1191): core "around 750 m" long, mean width 175 m; market streets now 17–18 m but originally about 27 m; 1389 registers list ≈1,620 houses, ≈820 in this core [5]. The Bernese *Hofstatt* was 60 × 100 ft at a 29.3 cm foot (≈17.6 × 29.3 m); the plan is a cross of streets with a *Stadtbach* running down the market street [6]. Freiburg was planned c.1100, its market street laid out in 1120 and its *Bächle* built c.1170, separate from drinking water; Rottweil has two crossing market streets, Landshut (1204) two street-markets [7]; Freiburg's market street is ≈900 m long and wide because the weekly market was held in it [8]; the citizens bought their freedom in 1368 for 20,000 marks of silver [9].
- San Giovanni Valdarno, the first Florentine *terra nuova*: walled rectangle 800 × 321 *braccia* (48.63 cm → ≈389 × 156 m), built core 450 × 226 br; main street 20 br (≈9.7 m), cross streets 10 br (≈4.9 m), a 20-br ring road inside the wall, a 37.5-br ditch-and-road outside; blocks 160–170 br long and 70 or 45 br deep (shallower toward the walls); standard lot 10 br (≈4.9 m) wide; square 80 br wide [10]. Scholars attribute the plan to Arnolfo di Cambio: a main street gate to gate, a perpendicular cross street, the square at the crossing [11].
- New Winchelsea: plots taken up July 1288; 802 plots and 690 holders in 1292; c.1300 area "151 acres / 611,500 m²", overall 4,000 × 1,800 ft (1,219 × 550 m) [12]; commission 1282, storm 1287, site handed over 1288, move complete by 1292; king's rent £14 11s 5d a year; ≈50 cellars (some 30 × 15 ft); population "said" to be 6,000 [13]. Caernarfon's 1298 plots were "sixty feet wide and eighty feet deep"; the 1292 Winchelsea surveyors prove "highly accurate" against GPS [14].

### 1.2 Renaissance ideal cities

- Sforzinda (Filarete, c.1464): an eight-point star of two overlaid squares in a circular moat, towers at the points, eight gates, radial avenues to market squares, three principal squares (palace, cathedral, market), a canal on every other street; never built [15]. Pienza stopped at Pius II's death in 1462 with "only 40 completed buildings" on a geometry "based on the numbers 3, 5, and 9" [16].
- Palmanova (7 October 1593; Scamozzi, Savorgnan advising): nine-pointed star around a six-sided square, three gates, nine ravelins, bastions and lunettes, eighteen cavaliers; first circuit "7 kilometres" round, 30 years to build; second phase 1658–90, Napoleonic works 1806–13; because "no one chose to move there", by 1622 Venice offered pardoned criminals free lots and materials; population today 5,301 [17]; streets run straight from the gates to the piazza [18]. Ferrara's Addizione Erculea (1492, Ercole I, Biagio Rossetti) "more than doubled the size of the walled town" behind walls once 13 km long, to house growth and resist Venice and the Papacy [19].

### 1.3 Street widths and squares

- Organic medieval towns: main roads "often only twelve to fifteen feet wide" (≈3.7–4.6 m), secondary roads "no wider than eight to ten feet" (≈2.4–3 m); walled areas 50–200 acres, Cologne almost 1,000; 13th-century German cities averaged 2,000–3,000 people and at most fifty exceeded 5,000; 14th-century London 30,000, Paris 70,000–80,000 [20]. Planned widths are 2–3× that: bastides 6–10 m [1], Monpazier 8 m [2], Grenade 8.55/7.02 m [3], SGV ≈9.7/4.9 m [10], Bern ≈27 m [5]; Paris's Rue Saint-Jacques (the Roman cardo) is 16–20 m today, 1,550 m long [21].
- Siena's Campo is 425 × 650 ft, paved in 1349 with eight lines dividing it into nine segments for the Nine (1292–1355), falling about 15 ft north to south, with eleven access points, and "large enough to hold the entire population when it was built, about 50,000 people" [22]. Kraków's Rynek (1257, Magdeburg law) covers 3.79 ha with three evenly spaced streets leaving each side [23]. Bruges's market hall is 44 × 84 m under an 83 m belfry (102 m before 1741) begun c.1240 that held the treasury and archives and served as a fire lookout [24].
- **Ratio (COMPUTED):** Siena ≈2.6 ha / 50,000 ≈ 0.5 m² per inhabitant; Monpazier's ≈0.3 ha for ≈2,250 ≈ 1.3 m². Small towns are over-squared because the square *is* the market.

### 1.4 Guild districts, noxious trades and building law

- York barred tanners from washing limed skins upstream of Pudding Holes; in 1371 butchers were banned from dumping offal between Ouse Bridge and the Friars Minor wharf and sent to a downstream pier, with a 100-shilling fine within 200 ft of the friary (1372) and a 6d fine (1377); Hungate became the official dump in 1524; a shipwright was paid £10 a year in 1571 to dredge the Ouse to 7 ft. Coventry (1421) fined dung-dumping half a mark, one mark and 20 shillings on successive offences and appointed river overseers for four sections (1426). Norwich paid river-cleaning supervisors from 1367, licensed a single muck-carrier in 1453, taxed polluting trades more heavily in 1532 (barkers, dyers, parchment-makers, saddlers, brewers, sheep-washers) and in 1552 set up a twelve-man "Surveyours of the Ryver and Streates", funded partly by £14 a year from the mills, that lasted into the 1800s [25]. York's Tanner Row lies by the Ouse, "Barker Rawe" in 1524 [26].
- London's Assize of Buildings (1189): joint party walls 3 ft thick and 16 ft high (each neighbour giving 1.5 ft), stone-lined cesspits 2.5 ft and unlined ones 3.5 ft from the neighbour's land, joint gutters or discharge onto one's own land or the king's highway; after the 1130 fire stone houses "covered with thick tiles" were encouraged [27]. Genoa capped towers at 80 ft; late-medieval London had "at least sixteen public latrines" for ≈25,000–30,000 people [20].

### 1.5 Walls, gates, suburbs and density

- Florence: 25,000 people in 1125, 80,000 in 1280, ≈100,000 in the early 1300s; the second communal circuit (2 January 1285 – 1333) ran 8,500 m, 6 m high, with 63 towers and 12 gates each with antegate and drawbridge, enclosing 430 ha, "five times larger than the previous enclosure", triggered by "significant demographic and construction expansion" [28]. The 1172 circuit had crossed the Arno and created six *sestieri*; 1343 brought four *quartieri*; the 1333 wall held over 80,000 people plus farmland [29].
- Paris: Philip Augustus's wall (1190–1213) enclosed 253 ha, king-funded; Charles V (1356–83) 439 ha; the Farmers-General tax wall (1784–91) 3,402 ha; Thiers (1841–46) 7,802 ha [30]; the 1190 wall was 6–10 ft thick and up to 30 ft tall with a tower every 200 ft and six gates a side [20]. Cologne: 20,000 in 1140, 40,000 in 1180, still 40,000 in 1430 and 1600; Roman walls 96.8 ha; the 1180 wall's area "has not changed for more than 600 years" until 1794 [31]. Bruges: ≈75 ha inside the early-12th-century walls, ≈370 ha inside the 1297 "egg" for ≈45,000 residents [44]; that line is "more than six kilometres" long with nine stone gates and carried 25–30 windmills [43].
- Density scaling: in 173 cities c.1280–1320 settled area grows as population^0.71–0.79, so "larger settlements have higher population densities"; Bristol's area was 130 ha [32]. **COMPUTED:** Monpazier ≈255/ha; Florence 1333 ≈230/ha; Paris 1213 ≈280–315/ha; Bruges ≈120/ha; Cologne ≈100/ha; Winchelsea 6,000 / 61 ha ≈ 98/ha.

### 1.6 What "upgrading" meant institutionally

- England and Wales had 2,393 places with markets or fairs by 1516; 15 % of markets (5 % of fairs) are recorded by 1200 against 71 % / 59 % by 1300; 57 % of markets and 54 % of fairs were created in the 13th century; three-quarters of markets and 86 % of fairs rested on royal grants; 6⅔ miles was the mid-13th-century rule for a "neighbouring" market; a market was weekly, a fair yearly at a religious festival; 72 % of 1200's market places were still market towns in 1600, but only 39 % of 1300's [33]. Domesday lists 50 markets; ≈2,000 were added 1200–1349; spacing was "a day's worth of travelling (approximately 10 kilometres)"; pre-1199 markets were "prescriptive" [34].
- Burgage tenure: Tewkesbury's primary plots were 4 × 40 perches (1 acre), later cut into perch-wide strips; a burgess paid a fixed money rent and a royal charter of incorporation as a "free borough" let freeholders buy and sell [36]. City status in England meant a diocesan cathedral until the 16th century; Henry VIII's six new dioceses (1540s) made six cities; Wells kept the title with 10,000 people; Portsmouth was refused in 1911; 66 UK cities in 2004 [35]. Lübeck: founded 1143, Henry the Lion 1158, imperial free city 1226, ≈18,800 people in 1350 and 25,444 in 1502, "Queen of the Hanseatic League" [42].

### 1.7 Neighbourhoods: parishes, quarters, contrade

- Siena: 17 contrade today of an original 59; Violante of Bavaria's 1729 edict abolished six and "defined the boundaries… which are still valid"; each has "its own museum, church, fountain and baptismal font, motto, allied contrada… and adversary contrada", having begun as recruiting units for military companies [37].
- Venice c.1586–87: 148,640 people in six sestieri (Castello 31,066, San Marco 23,657, Cannaregio 34,311, San Polo 11,381, Santa Croce 16,777, Dorsoduro 31,448); Castello lists 13 churches, San Polo 9, Santa Croce 8, Dorsoduro 11; San Marco had 6,594 households [39] — ≈1,260–2,400 people per parish (COMPUTED). The parish cistern was the cell's utility: 147 public wells in 1581, 157 in 1795, 160 public + 140 semi-public by the early 1800s, ≈2,700 private cisterns c.1500; public cisterns held ≈50 *botti* (0.8463 m³ each), private ≈30; they opened "twice a day" by bell with the *capi contrada* holding the keys; supply averaged 5.5–6 L per person per day, ≈1.4 L for the poor on public cisterns [38].
- Florence from 1343: four quartieri × four gonfaloni = 16; each gonfalone's captain led a militia company, collected taxes and sat on a sixteen-man council [29] — ≈5,000–6,000 people each (COMPUTED). London in the 1170s had 126 parish churches in about a square mile for a peak 40,000–50,000: "roughly one church for every 300–400 residents" [40]; Norwich ≈58 churches for ≈10,000 people (walls 1280–1340); York 47 parishes, Lincoln 47, Winchester 57, Exeter 29, Oxford 20, Canterbury 17; 11th-century towns have many small parishes, 12th-century ones a single large one [41].

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§1)

- **Street classes.** Keep the 13-block corridor as the planned main street (Bern 27 m clamps to 13; SGV/Monpazier 8–10 m → 9–11 of roadway); add a **5-block side street** (SGV 4.9 m, bastide 5–6 m) and a **2–3-block alley**. Organic tiers spawn 3–5-block streets; planned tiers 7–13.
- **Plot module.** Planned lot **5 wide × 25 deep** (SGV 10 br; bastide 8 × 24; Grenade 9 × 27.5), depth shrinking toward the wall (70 → 45 br ≈ 34 → 22 m); Zähringer merchant lot **18 × 29**; a church reserves **12 lots**.
- **Square.** Side = clamp(0.5 × √population, 25, 200): a 2,000-person town gets ≈50 × 50 (bastide), a 50,000 city ≈130 × 200 (Siena), capped near Kraków's 3.79 ha; three streets per side or two per corner.
- **Noxious trades.** Tanner/dyer/butcher lots ≥ **60 blocks** (200 ft) from churches, on the downstream half of the river edge or outside the wall; fines escalate 1 → 2 → 40 units. Fire law: 1-block stone party wall every two houses; cesspit ≥ 3 blocks from the neighbour.
- **Walls.** New circuit when density passes **≈200/ha** or suburbs hold > 25 % of houses; each rebuild multiplies area by **2–5**; one gate per **≈700 blocks** of wall, a tower every **60–135 blocks**. Density targets: village 20–60/ha; market town 100–150; walled city 200–300; size the next circuit with area ≈ k·pop^0.75.
- **Institutional ladder (one per tier).** T1 hamlet → T2 chapel + well → T3 **market charter** (weekly market; no rival within 1,000 blocks ≈ 6⅔ miles scaled) → T4 borough (burgage plots, fixed rents, wall licence) → T5 annual fair + stone bridge → T6 city (cathedral) → T7 capital/port (fondaco, arsenal) → T8 metropolis (second circuit, 16 gonfaloni).
- **Neighbourhood cell.** One cell per **300–400 people** in organic 11th-century-style towns, **1,200–2,500** in planned or Venetian towns, **5,000–6,000** per Florentine gonfalone; each gets a church, a well or cistern opened twice a day by bell, an oven, a small square, and (cities) a militia banner. Fix boundaries once (Siena 1729); growth adds cells rather than redrawing them.

## 2. Terrain: hill towns, terraces, stairs, houses on slopes and over water

- **Houses over water.** Santa Maria della Salute (1631–87) stands on "a platform made of 1,000,000 wooden piles" and measures 70 × 47 m; the plague that prompted it killed 46,000 in the city, "nearly a third of the population" [45]. Venetian alder piles were "hammered about 4 meters deep", with oak or larch where water resistance mattered; 116 islands, 177 canals, 423 bridges [46]. Amsterdam's Royal Palace (1648–65) rests on 13,659 piles for 22,031 m² of floor [47]; Central Station sits on 8,687 and the Concertgebouw on 2,186; ground level in 1325 was "more than 5 meters" lower than today; brick was mandatory from 1669 [48].
- **Stilt houses.** The Alpine pile dwellings (c.5000–500 BC) comprise 111 inscribed sites out of 937 known, in six countries (56 in Switzerland), with cart axles from c.3400 BC [49]; stilt houses worldwide are "primarily built to protect against flooding", to keep out vermin, and the shaded space beneath is used for work and storage [50]. No source gave a standard floor height; this remains unfilled.
- **Hill towns.** The Italian hill-town sources consulted were qualitative (streets "built for feet", San Gimignano's fourteen surviving towers); no slope percentages or street-width statistics were found for Perugia, Assisi, Gubbio or Orvieto, so hill-town geometry below is drawn from the US and road-engineering sources instead.
- **Stairs and ramps.** Blondel (1675): going + 2 × rise = 24 in (610 mm); current UK rules for private stairs give rise 150–220 mm, going 220–300 mm, pitch ≤ 42°, and 2r + g = 550–700 mm [51]. Telford's Holyhead Road (1815–26) kept grades "never exceeding 1 in 17 (5.9%)" against the old road's 1 in 6; 85 of its miles are in Wales [54]. The Valley Turnpike (1840) limited grade to 3% for horse and mule carts, with an 18-ft macadam surface and toll gates every five miles [52]; McAdam's 30-ft road rose only 3 in from edge to crown, over 8 in of ≤3-in stone and a 2-in top course [53].
- **Slope regulation (APA PAS 126, 1968).** Fremont allowed hillside streets up to 20 % grade on a 40 ft right-of-way / 26 ft pavement; Redding tied lot size to cross-slope (≤20 %: 6,000–10,000 sq ft; ≤25 %: 10,000–20,000; ≤30 %: 20,000+; ≤40 %: 1+ acre); Santa Barbara capped cuts at 1.5 : 1 and fills at 2 : 1 with 75-ft curve radii; Los Angeles County kept buildings 5 ft from a fill crest [55].

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§2)

- **Grade ladder.** Cart street ≤ **1 in 17** (Telford) → 6 % → at most 1 block rise per 17 blocks run; the existing 1-in-7 ramp (14 %) is a *pedestrian/pack* grade, legal for mules and hand-carts but not wagons; anything steeper becomes a **stair street**. Streetcar/incline tier (Tier 6+) tolerates 15–25 % (Duluth's incline range, §3) only as a funicular prefab.
- **Stair prefab.** The Minecraft stair block rises 1 per 1 going (45°, above any code's 42° pitch cap) and breaks Blondel (2 × 1,000 + 1,000 = 3,000 mm vs 610); a stair-then-slab run (1 rise per 2 going) is closer but still ladder-grade. Compensate with a **landing of ≥ 3 blocks every 6 risers**, width ≥ 3 blocks, railings both sides above 4 risers.
- **Cut-and-fill law.** Cut slopes ≤ 1.5 : 1 (3 blocks horizontal per 2 vertical), fills ≤ 2 : 1; a building footprint sits ≥ 5 blocks back from a fill crest; terrace walls ≤ 4 blocks high (Cinque-Terre scale dry-stone; no numeric source found, so this is a proposal).
- **Lot size by slope.** 0–10 %: 5 × 25 lots (§1); 10–20 %: 5 × 30 with a 1-block terrace per 5 blocks of depth; 20–30 %: 8 wide, half as many; > 30 %: no lots — stairs, retaining walls, orchards (Redding's ladder).
- **Over-water tier.** Pile houses need a pile field at **1 pile per block** of footprint, 4 blocks deep (Venice), on a stone plinth 1 block above high water; a canal house 7 blocks wide ≈ 100–200 piles. Stilt houses: floor 2 blocks above ground, workspace below.

## 3. Northeast / Mid-Atlantic / Rust-Belt US hill cities — the "stepped town" model

### 3.1 Pittsburgh

- Pittsburgh owns "nearly 800 sets" of city steps; Bob Regan's catalogue (book 2004) lists 739 sets, 44,645 risers and 24,108 vertical feet; Ernie Pyle (1937) counted "nearly 15 miles"; ≈450 sets are free-standing structures and 350 are built into sidewalks; the steps are legally **city streets** ("paper streets") with names and signs; nearly two-thirds lie in low- or moderate-income areas [56]. The earliest were built "between the late 1800s and early 1900s" as "the first form of public transportation for the steel workers who lived in the hills to commute down to the mill" [57]. Steps appear on Sanborn maps of 1884; the city built them "from 1911 (and probably earlier) into the 1950s"; the Joncaire steps descend "130-some treads" and cost $770,000 to renovate (2018); the steps budget was $200,000 a year before 2019 and $1.43 million in 2019; the South Side Slopes' 68 flights are "more than double that of any other neighborhood" [58]. Most surviving stairways "were built in the 1940s and 1950s", first in wood and later rebuilt in concrete; one set can cost $100,000 to repair [59]; 66 of the city's 90 neighbourhoods contain steps and the Armstrong steps (1911) are the oldest listed [60].
- South Side Slopes: ≈70 staircases, elevations 791–1,174 ft (241–358 m) in 0.716 sq mi; J&L Steel below employed 15,000 by 1910; workers' houses were "one room wide, two rooms deep, and up to four stories high"; population fell from 15,083 (1940) to 5,007 (2010) [61].
- Pittsburgh built 17 inclines between 1870 (Monongahela) and 1901 (Norwood); two survive [62]. The Monongahela Incline (opened 28 May 1870) is 635 ft long with a 369.39 ft rise at 35° 35′, runs 6 mph with 23 passengers per car, carried 944 fares on day one and 4,174 on day two, and was proposed by German immigrants who remembered the *Seilbahnen* of home [63]. The Duquesne (17 May 1877) is 793–800 ft long, rises 400 ft at 30°, runs 4.03 mph with 18–25 per compartment; the two together carried over a million riders a year by 1977 [64]. A freight incline beside the Monongahela carried wagons from 31 March 1884 to 1935; the Duquesne's grade is quoted as 58 % [65]. Canton Avenue (Beechview) reaches 37 % over 21 ft of a 630-ft street [66].
- **Not found:** riser height, tread depth and landing spacing standards for the city steps; typical lot widths on the Slopes. The housing description (one room wide, up to four storeys) implies frontages of ≈4–5 m, consistent with the 20–25 ft urban lot, but no Pittsburgh source stated it.

### 3.2 Cincinnati

- Cincinnati owns "roughly 400 sets of steps"; the first were laid by Mount Auburn residents in the 1830s; at the 19th-century peak there were "over 30 miles" of hillside steps; the Immaculata steps (85 + 65, rebuilt in concrete 1911, 96 after the 2009 reconstruction) have carried a Good Friday pilgrimage since 1860 [67]. The Mount Adams Incline (opened 8 March 1876, third of five, closed 1948) was 945 ft long and in 1947 took $8,407 in fares against $68,617 in costs [68]; the Price Hill Incline (1874–1943) was 800 ft long and climbed 350 ft, converted from steam to electric in 1923 [69]. Horsecars ran from 14 September 1859 but "the hills were impossible to climb in bad weather"; electric cars from 17 August 1889 reached 222 route-miles and over 100 million passengers a year [70].

### 3.3 Providence, Boston, Albany, Yonkers

- Providence's Benefit Street was laid out in 1756 "midway up the hill paralleling the Town Street" as the "Back Street", the first highway to cut across the proprietors' long lots; its houses use raised basements and terraces [71]; the College Hill district listed 348 contributing structures in 1960 and ≈945 properties after the 1977/1990 expansions [72].
- Boston's Mount Vernon Proprietors bought an 18.5-acre pasture for $18,450 [74] in 1795, cut down Mount Vernon in 1803–05 and used the fill for Charles Street, imposed a 30-ft setback on Mount Vernon Street, built houses from 1799–1802 and laid out Louisburg Square in 1826 as their last act [73]. No source consulted gave the street grades.
- Albany's State Street was a 150-ft-wide boulevard running uphill from the Hudson past the fort to Eagle Street, with Pearl Street dropping into the Foxes Creek ravine "lined with working class homes" [75].
- Yonkers' Park Hill (developed 1888 by the American Real Estate Company, on a "300-foot plateau") was served by the Park Hill Incline (1894–1937) from the Getty Square railway branch to Alta Avenue [76] — a "107-foot, 40-degree" funicular with a single ten-passenger car [77].

### 3.4 Manayunk, Worcester and the mill towns

- Manayunk: the canal section was finished at the end of 1818, the first water-powered mill opened in 1819, and the 1827 census counted 147 families and 1,098 people [80]; the "Wall" on Levering Street/Lyceum Avenue peaks at a 17 % grade [78]; workers lived in attached 2–3-storey row houses (3-storey, 2-bay at 104–106 Levering) and "north of Main Street the topography rises steeply to Ridge Avenue" [79].
- Worcester's seven hills are Airport, Bancroft, Belmont (Bell), Grafton, Green, Pakachoag and Vernon; the three-decker originated there (Francis Gallagher, 1830–1911); the Blackstone Canal opened in 1828; city elevation 479 ft [81].
- Lowell: brick boardinghouses multiplied from the mid-1830s; "typically 30 to 40 young women lived together in a boardinghouse", four to eight to a bedroom, two to a bed [82]. Manchester NH: Elm Street "ran atop a ridge parallel to the mills below, but at a remove to lessen their clamor"; six blocks of boarding houses were built in 1839; the mills covered 5,844,340 sq ft on ≈137 acres and employed 17,000 at peak [83]; the corporation-housing district (1838–50) has seventeen rowhouse tenement blocks on ≈170 acres, "oriented east–west (perpendicular to the canals and the mill buildings), with the overseer housing set crosswise" [84]; company housing lay between Elm, Canal, Hollis and Pleasant Streets and was reserved for overseers, second hands and skilled workers; 1917 infant mortality was 211.4 per 1,000 in the poorest housing against a city average of 165 [85].
- Paterson: 77-ft falls; S.U.M. 1791; L'Enfant and Peter Colt designed the first raceway [87]; 1815: eleven cotton mills and 74 dwellings; 1832: 9,000 people, 841 dwellings, 20 mills, the Dublin quarter holding 48.6 % of the population in houses "built directly adjacent to the mills on a series of streets laid out in a square, compact unit"; 20,000 by 1860, ≈100,000 by 1900 [86]. Holyoke (1847–92): 4.5 miles of canal on three levels (140 ft × 22 ft deep down to ≈100 ft × 10 ft), 6,000 cfs ≈ 30,000 hp "enough to power 450 mills", 28 bridges [88]; the Hadley Falls company houses (1847–48) are five brick rows of 8–10 units, 2½ storeys, on 2.1 acres [89].

### 3.5 Duluth, and San Francisco / Seattle as comparison

- Duluth "is built into the side of a hill that rises 600 feet in a matter of a mile or less"; its steepest street, Fifth Avenue West, is 25.5 % ("more than 100 feet over a single block"), 19th Avenue West 25.3 %; San Francisco's steepest are 31 %, Canton Avenue 37 % [90]. The 7th Avenue West Incline (1891–1939) ran 0.5 mile with a 509-ft rise at 15–25 % grade, cars for 250 standing passengers, 16 minutes one way, 2,170 weekday riders in 1925 [91].
- San Francisco's Lombard block had "a natural 27 percent grade" until the 1922 switchback of eight turns over ≈600 ft (412.5 ft straight-line) at a recommended 5 mph [92]; the Clay Street Hill Railroad (1 September 1873) climbed "348 feet in six city blocks" and carried 3,000 daily riders within a year, inspired by horses suffering on Jackson Street [93].
- Seattle removed 11,112,025 cubic yards from Denny Hill (originally ≈220 ft, lowered >100 ft) in five regrades 1897–1930 for $2,261,800 over ≈65 blocks / 200 acres; a hydraulic giant moved ≈1,000 cu yd per eight-hour shift [94]; the city made ≈60 regrades (≈50 million short tons), the Jackson Regrade (1907–10) cut 85 ft and the Dearborn cut 108 ft moving 1.6 million cu yd [95].

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§3)

- **Stair-street density.** Pittsburgh ≈ 739 sets / 90 neighbourhoods ≈ 8 per neighbourhood, 60 risers and 33 ft (≈10 m) rise per set on average (COMPUTED from [56]); Cincinnati 400 sets. Rule: in any hillside neighbourhood where slope > 14 %, spawn **one public stair per 60–80 m of contour street**, each averaging **30–35 risers** with landings every 6–10, connecting a contour street to the one above.
- **Contour-and-switchback grid.** Lay streets along contours at a vertical spacing equal to **one house height (8–12 blocks)**; connect them with (a) a cart switchback at ≤ 1 in 17 (so a 10-block rise needs ≥ 170 blocks of road, folded into 2–3 legs on a 75-block-radius curve) and (b) stair streets taking the fall line. Commercial strips sit on the **lowest contour street next to the mill/river** (Manayunk Main Street, Pittsburgh's Carson Street) and on the **ridge street set one block back from the mills** (Manchester's Elm Street).
- **Mill-town section (Tier 4–6).** River → mill yard (Amoskeag No. 11: 900 × 103 ft ≈ 270 × 31 m) → canal → corporation rows perpendicular to it (8–10 units, 2½ storeys, 30–40 boarders each) → ridge street → row houses "one room wide, two deep, up to four storeys" (5 × 10 lots) stepping uphill → stairs → incline at Tier 6.
- **Inclines.** Funicular prefab 190–290 long, 110–120 rise (Pittsburgh's survivors), 23 per car at ≈2.7 blocks/s; a freight variant when a hilltop district passes ≈1,000 residents (Monongahela freight incline, 1884); retire it when a ≤ 1-in-17 road reaches the top (Cincinnati 1943–48).
- **Grade caps.** Surfaced streets ≤ **25 %** (Duluth); building-fronting streets ≤ 20 % (Fremont); steeper → stairs, or a Lombard **switchback of 8 turns** over ≈180 blocks for a 27 % block.
- **Regrade option (Tier 7+).** Lower a hill at ≈1,000 blocks per crew-shift (Seattle's hydraulic giant), spoil to the shoreline fill zone.

## 4. Water, sanitation and sewers

### 4.1 Drains, cesspits and the men who emptied them

- Rome's Cloaca Maxima (traditionally c.600 BC, Tarquinius Priscus) grew to 1,600 m by the late Republic with a 101 m covered canal by the 2nd century BC, passed up to 10 m³/s and discharged near the Ponte Rotto; the Forum was drained with 10–20,000 m³ of fill [96].
- Siena's *bottini* total 25 km of tunnels; the Fonte Gaia branch delivers 2.7 L/s and the Fontebranda branch 3.5 L/s; the water channel is 20–30 cm wide in a 50–90 cm corridor 1.5–2 m high, at a constant gradient of **1 ‰ (1 m per km)**; first documented 1226, Fontebranda first mentioned 1081; the Fonte Gaia project was commissioned 16 December 1334 and water reached the Campo in 1343; a *dado* of water was ≈400 L per 24 h [97]. A *gorello* channel runs in the walkway floor; the last major extension was 1466 and the system was superseded by the 1914 Monte Amiata aqueduct [98].
- Gong farmers were paid "two shillings per ton" in the late 15th century and sixpence a day at Hampton Court under Elizabeth I; they could work only between 9 pm and 5 am; cesspits needed emptying "every two years or so"; late-14th-century London had "only 16" public latrines for 30,000 people; waste went to laystalls on the town edge (Dung Wharf; Mount Pleasant covered 7.5 acres by 1780); Richard the Raker drowned in a cesspit in 1325; Manchester in 1615 had nineteen rakers under two scavengers [99]. London's 1189 cesspit set-backs (2.5 ft lined / 3.5 ft unlined) are in §1.4 [27].

### 4.2 Nineteenth-century sewers

- London: six main interceptors "totalling almost 100 miles", fed by 450 miles of main sewers and 13,000 miles of local sewers, built mainly 1859–65 from 318 million bricks, 2.7 million m³ of excavation and 670,000 m³ of concrete, sized for 6.5 mm/h of rainfall, with pumping stations at Chelsea, Deptford and Abbey Mills, all triggered by the Great Stink of 1858 [100]. The London Museum gives 82 miles of new intercepting sewers, 1,100 miles of street drains, Parliament's initial £2.5 million, opening in 1865 and completion in 1875; bricklayers' wages rose 20 % [101]; the ICE counts 1,300 miles of sewers in all and 52 acres reclaimed by the embankments (Albert 1868, Chelsea 1874, Victoria 1879) [102].
- Paris: the first vaulted sewer was built under rue Montmartre in 1370 by Hugues Aubriot; Belgrand took charge in 1855; the network reached 600 km in 1878; 68 % of buildings were connected by 1914 and over 1,000 km were added 1914–77; Belgrand gave galleries a slope of "3 cm per meter" so that men could walk and flush them, and some 4,000 flushing reservoirs existed by 1900 [103]. Napoleon's network was ≈30 km; today's is 2,400 km [104]. **Not found:** égoutier wages and hours, gallery cross-sections.
- Manhole spacing: historic US business districts 300–400 ft (91–122 m); modern US 400 ft for small sewers and 500 ft for mains; England max 90 m (200 m for large sewers); Scotland 100 m; openings 90–120 cm; inlet–outlet drop ≤ 60 cm [105].

### 4.3 Wells, conduits, fountains, baths, hospitals

- London's Great Conduit (authorised 1236) piped Tyburn water "a mile or more" in lead to Cheapside; twelve conduits were eventually built; water-carriers with two 3-gallon tubs numbered, with families, ≈4,000 c.1600; Morice's wheel (1580) raised 132,120 gallons an hour to 120 ft; the New River (1609–13) ran ≈60 km, 10 ft wide and 4 ft deep [106].
- Exeter's first external supply dates from the late 12th century; the Black Friars built theirs 1248–59; the cathedral paid £12 in 1347 to breach the wall for its pipe; the city's New Conduit (1420–24) got a salaried plumber in 1425 and a £150 remodelling in 1441 (£84 from donations); the cathedral passages took 1,850 loads of stone [107].
- Venice's cisterns were dug 3–4 m below high tide, lined with half a metre of clay and filtered through Lido sand; 50 were decreed in 1322 and 30 more in 1424; in 1596–98 33 barges made 11,857 trips in 16 months bringing Brenta water at 16 lire a load (see §1.7) [38].
- Bath-houses: Henry II's 1161 ordinance regulated the Bankside stews; 18 stood there c.1500; the City banned the trade within its walls in 1393 and Henry VIII closed Bankside in 1546; quarterly inspections [108].
- Hospitals: the Hôtel-Dieu of Paris (traditionally 651, first records 829) grew from 303 to ≈500 beds in the 15th–16th centuries while holding ≈700 patients (over 1,500 in crises) and admitting ≈25,000 a year; beds 1.3 m wide took two or three patients head-to-heel; by the 17th–18th centuries 1,200 beds held 2,000–4,000 patients and mortality neared 25 %; fire destroyed much of it in 1772 [109]. Florence's Ospedale degli Innocenti was commissioned in 1419, built 1419–27 by Brunelleschi with a nine-bay loggia, opened in 1445 and took its first foundling on 5 February 1445; it spent ≈100,000 lire a year, ran debts of 300,000–700,000 lire, and dismissed boys at eighteen [110]; wet-nurses kept infants to age two and the foundling window closed in 1875 [111]. St Bartholomew's was founded in 1123 by Rahere and refounded in December 1546; by 1872 it had 676 beds, 6,000 in-patients and 101,000 out-patients a year [112].

### 4.4 Order of acquisition and the mortality link

- **Order (COMPUTED from the dates and populations above):** wells/cisterns and a public latrine first (Venice 1322; London's 16 latrines at 30,000); a piped conduit at ≈10,000–40,000 people (Exeter c.1200 and 1420–24; London 1236; Siena 1226/1334); a hospital with the cathedral or guilds (Paris 829; London 1123; Florence 1419); a vaulted sewer only in a capital (Paris 1370); full sewerage only after mass cholera (London 1858).
- Mortality: Black Death estimates run from one in three (Ziegler) to 65 % (Benedictow: 52 of 80 million); Florence fell from 110,000–120,000 to 50,000 by 1351; Paris ≈50 %; Hamburg and Bremen ≥60 %; London more than a third [113]. Hamburg in August 1892 lost ≈10,000 of ≈800,000 to cholera in about six weeks because it "refused to spend anything to treat its city water" while neighbours filtered theirs [114]. Venice's 1630–31 plague killed 46,000 in the city [45].

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§4)

- **Water ladder by tier.** T1–2: one well per 25 households (Venice ≈1 public cistern per 1,000 people). T3: a public fountain on a gravity conduit falling **1 block per 1,000** (bottini 1 ‰ — effectively level in Bedrock, marked by shafts every 50 blocks). T4: a walkable 1 × 2 maintenance tunnel (bottini 0.5–0.9 × 1.5–2 m). T6: a culvert under the main street with shafts every **90–120 blocks** (manhole spacing) falling 1 block per 33 (Belgrand's 3 cm/m).
- **Cesspit law.** One privy per house, pit ≥ 3 blocks from the neighbour, emptied every **2 game-years** by a night crew working 21:00–05:00 (ticks 15,000–23,000) to a laystall ≥ 50 blocks outside the gate; one public latrine per **2,000 people** (London's 16 for 30,000) is the medieval floor.
- **Hospital.** An infirmary at the cathedral tier with beds = population / 150 (Hôtel-Dieu's 500 beds for ≈200,000 is 1 : 400, but held 2–3 patients per bed); a foundling hospital at the guild-city tier.
- **Epidemic coupling.** Plague/cholera risk scales with (population density) × (1 − sewer coverage) × (shared-well fraction); unmitigated outbreaks remove 30–60 % (Black Death range) and a filtered/sewered city caps losses at ≈1 % (Hamburg 10,000 / 800,000). Sewer completion should take ≈15 years of game time at city scale (London 1859–75).

## 5. Night watch, lighting and boundaries

### 5.1 Watch, curfew and gates

- The Statute of Winchester (1285): from Ascension to Michaelmas every city keeps watch "by six men at each gate", every borough by twelve, every vill by six or four "according to the number of the inhabitants", all night from sunset to sunrise; gates of enclosed towns shut from sunset to sunrise; roads between market towns cleared so that "no ditch, underwood or bushes" stand within 200 ft either side; the hundred answers for robberies within 40 days; men of 15–60 keep arms by wealth [115]. Watches patrolled "from 9 or 10 pm until sunrise"; householders served by rotation and by the 1660s commonly paid substitutes; the 1737 Watch Act fixed pay at £13 a year; by the second quarter of the 18th century a watchman carried a staff and lantern [116], called the hour, watched for fire and checked doors [117]. **Not found:** a medieval per-night wage.
- Curfew bells: 8 pm was usual (Sandwich rang for ten minutes; Coleraine, Ruthin 8 pm; Derry 9 pm; Inverness 5 pm later 8), and Wallingford's 9 pm bell was a privilege dating from 1068 [118].

### 5.2 Street lighting

- Paris: by 1665–67, 2,736 lanterns lit 912 streets, "a light source at each end plus a third in the middle if it was a particularly long" street; the 1667 ordinance required lighting only from 1 November to 1 March, extended to nine months of the year by the early 18th century; a torchbearer cost 3 sous (1662) and the lantern tax was 300,000 livres a year [119].
- London: Mayor Barton (1417) ordered lanterns hung between Hallowtide and Candlemas; a 1716 Act required lights before each house from six to eleven o'clock under a one-shilling fine; Hemming (1685) proposed whale-oil lamps at every tenth house on moonless nights until midnight; the 1736 Act rated 14,014 houses for 4,200 lamps at £8,610 a year (41s per lamp per year, 14s 6d to install), charging households 3s 6d to 12s a year [120].

### 5.3 Wolves and herds

- King Edgar took 300 wolf pelts a year from Wales (957); King John paid 5 shillings for two wolves; Edward I set Peter Corbet to destroy wolves in five western counties; Scotland's 1427 law required three hunts a year between 25 April and 1 August; English wolves were extinct or very rare by Henry VII (1485–1509), Scotland's last was killed in 1680 (folklore says 1743) [121]. Solon paid 5 drachmas for a male wolf and 1 for a female; France killed ≈1,400 wolves a year in 1818–29; Sweden paid bounties from 1647 and killed its last wolf in 1966; Massachusetts set the first American bounty on 9 November 1630; in states with wolves they take ≈2.5 % of predated sheep [122]. Massachusetts paid 10 shillings per wolf killed within ten miles of a plantation (15 May 1645); Newbury paid 40s (1664–65), 20s adult / 10s cub (1680–81), £4 for two (1704), 40s (1714) and £5 (1715); the last recorded bounty is 1721–22 [123]; wolves have been extinct in the state since about 1840 [124].

### 5.4 Boundary stones and beating the bounds

- Rogationtide perambulations (Monday–Wednesday before Ascension) were restated by Elizabeth's 1559 Injunction 18 for "the curate and the substantialest men"; marks were stakes, mere-stones, trees and gospel oaks; Bassingbourn's 11-mile bounds took two days, Purton's ≈20 miles had 27 gospel readings, Long Melford's ran 21 miles; 150 boys walked St Martin-in-the-Fields' bounds in 1619 and were bumped or pinched at the marks to fix them in memory; Runwell and Rettendon came to blows in 1578 and 400 Colchester people levelled enclosures during the 1641 walk [127].
- New England: medieval England walked bounds every seven years; Connecticut required it yearly, then every three, then every five; New Hampshire every seven; Maine every ten (now only on dispute); Boston's 1981 walk found a street it ploughed was in Everett; the New Hampshire–Vermont line went to the US Supreme Court in 1937 and the states held their 12th joint perambulation in 2023 [125]. Massachusetts General Laws c.42 §2 still requires towns to locate their boundary markers every five years; Boston's 1896 committee of five inspected every monument with seven adjoining towns [126].

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§5)

- **Watch.** **6 men per gate (city) / 12 (borough) / 4–6 (village)** [115]; householders rotate, paid substitutes (13 units a year) above Tier 6; patrol the wall circuit and every street > 7 blocks from 21:00 to sunrise (ticks 15,000 → 0); hue-and-cry propagates village to village along the road graph and the district pays if no culprit is found in 40 days.
- **Curfew/gates.** Curfew bell at 20:00 game time (tick 14,000); gates close sunset → sunrise (tick 13,000 → 23,000 in Bedrock's day); markets are the only exception (Monpazier's gate count of six for 2,000 people shows gates scale with roads, not population).
- **Lamps.** From the borough tier: one lantern at **each end of every street and one mid-street when > 100 blocks** (Paris 1665: 2,736 / 912 ≈ 3 per street); lit 1 November–1 March at first, nine months at the next tier; lamp tax ≈2 units per lamp-year (£8,610 / 4,200 [120]); Hemming's "every tenth house" ≈ one lamp per **50 blocks of frontage**.
- **Wolves.** Bounty = **half a day-wage × 60** (10s when a labourer earned ≈2d) within 10,000 blocks of a settlement; livestock loss probability near forest edge ≈ 2.5 %/year per flock (modern US sheep figure) rising to ×5 when no shepherd dog or fold is present; wolves vanish from a region after ≈200 years of bounties (Massachusetts 1630 → 1840).
- **Boundaries.** Mete-stones at every road crossing and ridge; a perambulation every **5 game-years** (Massachusetts) re-renders them and fires a dispute check; disputes go joint survey → committee of 5 → purchase or compromise line; unresolved ones raise a feud flag that cuts trade. Commons outside the stones carry a stint of **2 cows + 6–10 sheep per cottage** [134].

## 6. Environmental conservation, historical

- **Venice.** The Great Council's 1350 law gave the Arsenal first choice of the finest oak on the market; Doge Steno first requisitioned Belluno timber in 1410; six forest laws of 1476 "placed the Venetians in direct control of all of Terraferma's community forests", with Montello and Vizza as oak reserves [128]. Cansiglio came under Venetian protection in 1420 as the *Bosco da Reme* (oar forest) for the Arsenal's beech, got its first magistrate "sopra legne e boschi" in 1548, and was ringed with stone *cippi* bearing the Forest Captain's initials [129]. **Not found:** reserve areas in hectares, oak counts and penalty scales.
- **Nuremberg and the German ordinances.** Peter Stromer (c.1310–1388) sowed conifer seed in ploughed furrows in the Reichswald from 1368 because the city's forges on the Pegnitz and its mines faced a "deteriorating timber supply"; "fir seeders" trained in Nuremberg carried the method to Frankfurt [132][131]. Fernow's chronology: Erfurt divided a 286-acre town forest into seven felling areas in 1359 (a 7-year rotation); Nuremberg sowed "several hundred acres" of burnt land with pine, spruce and fir in 1368; Frankfurt followed in 1420; Lorsch forbade clearings in 1165, Salzburg in 1237 "so the cut forest may grow up to wood again"; Brunswick (1488) set a 12-inch felling diameter and required 15 oaks or aspens per acre left as seed trees; Roman coppice ran oak 7 years, chestnut 5, willow 3; Germany was still 26 % forest in 1911 [130].
- **Coppice and pollard rotations.** Hazel 7-year cycle (hurdles); sweet chestnut 15 (fencing); oak/hornbeam 15–25 (firewood and building); first coppice at 5–6 years; cut only late September–early March; a 6-ft fence for at least two years against browsing; first pollard at 5–15 years; thin 20 % of trees every 5–8 years from year 10 (≈7 m tall) [133].
- **Commons.** About 50 % of England and Wales was common land in 1500 against ≈3 % today; Weston Turville's 1798 stint was "two cows, a bullock and six sheep" per right, a cottage right "two cows and perhaps 6–10 sheep", and one cow per 30 acres of open-field holding; by 1800 some 85 % of agricultural labourers had no common right [134].
- **London's green belt.** Elizabeth I banned new building within three miles of the City's gates in 1580 [136][135]; Loudon (1829) proposed alternating mile-wide zones of building and half-mile zones of country so no one lived more than half a mile from open ground; Howard's Garden City (1898) put 32,000 people on 6,000 acres with a 1,000-acre town [136]; the Metropolitan Green Belt was proposed in 1935, enacted 1938, idealised in 1955 at "7 to 10 miles deep", later stretching 35 miles out, and covers 513,860 ha — about three times London's area [135].
- **Quarries reused.** Syracuse's Latomia dei Cappuccini is 30–40 m deep; thousands of Athenian prisoners were held there after 413 BC; in 1582 it was granted to the Capuchins, who planted it as a garden [137]. Paris's quarries: the sources consulted gave the ossuary (over six million remains, opened 1810, 480,000 visitors in 2018) and the 1774 collapses but no tunnel length; the ≈300 km figure was not verified and is omitted.

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§6)

- **Reserved forest.** At the shipyard/arsenal tier the town rings a reserve of mature oak and beech with boundary stones; cutting inside is fined **100 day-wages**; the reserve is censused every 25 game-years and oaks need **≥ 100 game-years** to reach ship girth (a proposal — no numeric source).
- **Coppice coupes.** Divide each wood into **7 coupes** (Erfurt 1359) cut one a year for firewood, or **15–25** for construction oak; resow after fire (Nuremberg); leave **15 standards per acre (≈1 per 16 × 16 blocks)**; fence new coppice two years.
- **Pollards** along roads and commons: first cut at 10 years, then every 10–15.
- **Common land and stint.** Each cottage gets a grazing right of 2 cows + 8 sheep on the common; overstocking is fined by the manor court; the common shrinks from 50 % of the territory at Tier 1–3 to ≈ 3 % at Tier 8 unless a conservation policy is chosen.
- **Green belt.** Tier 7: no new houses within **300 blocks** beyond the wall (Elizabeth's 3 miles, scaled); Tier 8: Loudon's alternation (300 built / 150 open) keeps every house within 150 blocks of open ground.
- **Quarry afterlife.** A worked-out quarry ≥ 20 blocks deep becomes an orchard/garden lot after 50 game years (Latomia 1582), a storage cellar before that.

## 7. Environmental conservation, modern and futuristic

- **Wildlife crossings.** Banff: 44 structures (6 overpasses, 38 underpasses) along 82 km of 2.4 m fence; collisions down more than 80 % overall and 96 % for elk and deer; 11 large-mammal species, more than 150,000 crossings 1996–2012 [138]. Clevenger logged 185,683 crossings 1996–2009 (deer 62 %, elk 18 %, grizzly 679, a 35-fold rise), use "leveled out after an average of four to six years", carnivore road deaths were 50–100 % lower, and the newest overpasses are 60 m wide [139]. Worldwide 120 overpasses average 34 m; ≈50 m structures see double the use; grizzlies, elk and moose want open overpasses, cougars long low culverts [140]. The Dutch Natuurbrug Zanderij Crailoo (2006) is 800 m long and 50 m wide, one of over 600 ecoducts [141].
- **Urban growth boundaries.** Oregon's SB 100 (2 May 1973); Metro (1979) must keep a 20-year land supply and review its UGB every five years; the 2002 expansion added 18,867 acres; ≈240 UGB jurisdictions; Measure 49 passed with 62 % in 2006; urban reserves (2007) plan 40–50 years ahead [142].
- **Dark sky.** Flagstaff's first lighting ordinance (searchlights) dates from 1958; the 1989 code caps commercial lighting at 25,000 / 50,000 / 100,000 lumens per acre in Zones 1–3 (2.5, 7 and 35 miles from the observatories) and single-family lots at 10,000 everywhere, requires fully shielded fixtures and favours low-pressure sodium or narrow-spectrum amber LEDs; Flagstaff became the first International Dark Sky City on 24 October 2001 [143].
- **Green roofs.** Extensive roofs with 3–6 in of media retain 50–70 % of annual rainfall (74 % measured in Chicago with 3 in); storms under 0.5 in produce little or no runoff; media above 4–6 in adds little; 85 % total-suspended-solids removal [144].
- **Sponge cities.** Endorsed by Xi Jinping in December 2013, policy from 2014, 16 pilot cities in 2015, 15 more in 2016, 87 by 2017; target: 80 % of urban areas absorbing or reusing 70 % of rainfall, 20 % of urban land by 2020 and 80 % by 2030, at an estimated $230 billion with one-fifth from the national government [145].
- **3-30-300.** Homes, schools and workplaces should see 3 trees, sit in a neighbourhood with >30 % canopy and lie within 300 m of a park; across 2.5 million buildings in eight cities, fewer than 20 % passed the "30" test except in Singapore and Seattle, and in half the cities 25–40 % of buildings had 0–10 % canopy [146].
- **15-minute city.** Moreno (2016): daily needs within a 15-minute walk, ride or transit trip — ≈1 km at ≈4 km/h; adopted by Paris in 2020; Melbourne's 20-minute neighbourhoods (2017), Singapore's 20-minute towns / 45-minute city by 2040; a 5-minute neighbourhood is ≈400 m [147].
- **Rewilding and regenerative buildings.** Knepp (3,500 acres, from 2001): breeding-bird abundance +916 % (2007–25), nightingales 9 → 62 singing males, turtle doves +600 %, 283 purple emperors in one day (2025), invertebrate species 567 → 656, 96 ha of new scrub by 2019, under free-roaming cattle, ponies, pigs and deer [148]. The six-storey Bullitt Center runs on a 244 kW array, uses 152,877 kWh a year (EUI 10) with a 90,793 kWh surplus [149].

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§7)

- **Crossing law.** Any road or wall ≥ 500 blocks through forest gets a **3-block fence** (2.4 m) and one crossing per **2,000 blocks** (Banff ≈ 1 per 1.9 km): overpasses **50 wide** for large game, 3 × 3 culverts for small carnivores; spawn paths adopt them after 4–6 game years; collisions on fenced roads drop 80 %.
- **Ecoduct over the town's own barriers** (rail, canal, wall) at Tier 8: 50 wide × up to 800 long.
- **Growth boundary.** From Tier 5 the sim fixes a UGB holding a **20-year housing supply** (at the tier's growth rate) and reviews it every 5 game years; land outside stays farm/forest. Reserve a second ring for 40–50 years.
- **Lighting budget.** Lumens-per-acre caps translate to **lamp counts per 64 × 64 chunk**: residential 2, commercial inner 5, outer 10, all shielded (downward-facing) and warm (amber); a dark-sky policy lowers night-light spawn radius for mobs around the observatory lot.
- **Roofs and water.** Green roofs on ≥ 30 % of a block's roof area cut the block's runoff by ≈ 50–70 % in the drainage sim; a sponge-city policy targets 70 % of rain retained over 80 % of the town by Tier 8.
- **3-30-300 / 15-minute checks.** Each house sees 3 trees within 30 blocks, its chunk carries 30 % canopy, a park ≥ 50 × 100 lies within 300 blocks, and bakery, chapel, market and well lie within **1,000 blocks** (400 in the "5-minute" village tier).
- **Rewilding.** An abandoned farm cell turns to scrub in 20 game-years and reaches ×9 bird spawn weight by year 25 if grazers remain.

## 8. Roads between settlements and trade

- **Road law.** The Statute of Winchester (1285) ordered highways between market towns cleared of ditch, underwood and bushes for 200 ft on either side [115]. Medieval drove roads ran "20 metres across, with wide grazing verges", early-modern ones 40–90 ft between walls; overnight stances lay about 10 miles apart; drovers moved herds of 300–400; licensing began in 1563; 150,000 turkeys a year walked from East Anglia to London in three months; the last big Welsh drove was in 1870 [154]. Packhorse trains of 12–20 (up to 40) ponies carried ≈240 lb (110 kg) each in two panniers and made ≈25 miles a day; some 1,000 packhorses a day passed Clitheroe before 1750 [152].
- **Bridges.** Exeter's stone bridge (1190–1214) was 590 ft (some say 750) long, 16 ft wide with a 12-ft roadway and 17–18 arches, financed by £10,000 of public donations plus tolls on outside carts and rents from 15 bridge shops and 50 properties, and served until 1778 [155].
- **Speeds and loads.** Fourteenth-century walkers managed ≈30 miles a day in 8–10 hours; riders 30–40; relay messengers 52–56; Margaret of Brabant's five-horse baggage cart took 18 days for ≈85 miles (1297) [150]. Another compilation: riders 20–30 miles a day on long trips, 40 without duress, couriers 60 in 24 h; horse or mule wagons ≈20 miles a day, ox wagons 10–12, pack trains ≈10; a destrier carries 250–300 lb [151]. Medieval carts at the Tonnerre quarry averaged ≈15 cu ft (≈1 ton) per load; "a ton was a very respectable load for horse-hauled transport"; Roman law capped an *angaria* wagon at 1,500 Roman pounds; 26 pairs of oxen hauled church columns; a bombard needed 24 horses [153].
- **Fairs, leagues and safe roads.** The six Champagne fairs rotated through Lagny (2 January), Bar-sur-Aube, Provins (May and 14 September) and Troyes (hot fair after 24 June, cold fair 2 November), each running over six weeks — eight days to set up, then cloth, leather and spice days, and a final four days to settle accounts; the counts guaranteed merchants' security and contracts, their Guards of the Fair enforced them, and Philip Augustus granted safe conduct across France in 1209; fair taxes fell from 8,383 livres (1296) to 1,152 (1340–41) [156]. The Hansa (term 1267, first Diet 1356, last 1669) reached ≈200 settlements with a core of 70–170 towns and four Kontore (Novgorod, Bruges, Bergen to 1754, London Steelyard closed 1598); a Bremen cog carried ≈125 tons and 15th-century hulks up to 500; the Victual Brothers menaced the Baltic 1392–1430s [157]. Under the Pax Mongolica, yam relay stations stood "every 25–30 miles or an average day's journey on horse" from 1234, and "a maiden bearing a nugget of gold on her head could wander safely" [158].

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§8)

- **Highway prefab.** Cleared verge **60 blocks each side** (200 ft) between market towns; drove roads 20 wide between walls with grazing verges and a stance (pond + pen) every 10 miles — scaled to **1,000 blocks**; packhorse paths 2 wide.
- **Travel table (per game-day).** Walker 48 km (30 miles); horse 50–65; wagon 32; ox cart 16–19; pack train 16–40; courier 90. Cart load 1 ton ≈ 15 cu ft ≈ 0.4 m³ of stone — **two cartloads per block of ashlar** (a 1 m³ stone block ≈ 2.5 t).
- **Market spacing** 10 km (≈ 6⅔ miles) between weekly markets [33][34] → in CIVITAS, no two markets within 1,000 blocks; a fair once a year lasting 6 weeks (42 game days) at the regional tier.
- **Safe-road multiplier.** Trade volume between two towns = f(distance) × safety, where safety is 1.0 under a treaty/escort regime (Champagne conduit, Pax Mongolica yam), 0.5 with a night watch but no road guards, and 0.15 when pirates/bandits are active (Hansa tax collapse 8,383 → 1,152 ≈ ×0.14). Relay posts every 25–30 miles (≈1,000 blocks in sim) raise courier speed ×1.5.
- **Bridge economics.** A stone bridge costs 10,000 units (Exe) ≈ 5 years of a cathedral's budget; it pays back from tolls on outside carts and 15 shop rents; a wooden bridge first, stone at the fair tier.

## 9. The work day and material logistics

- **Hours and rest days.** Columella's year had 290 working days (45 lost to holidays and weather, 30 to rest after sowing); medieval estimates run ≈270, Kenyon's 308; sunrise-to-sunset gave ≈12.3 h of light and ≈10 h of work, 2,500–3,000 h a year, with 45 feast days plus Sundays [159]. Douai's shearers (1229) were paced at 4.5 cloths a day in winter and 6 in summer, with night work banned from 6 December to the Lagny fair; 16th-century Brussels changed hours eleven times a year between 7.5 h (winter) and 11.5 h (summer), Ghent 6.5–12.5 h [160].
- **Workforces and durations.** Beaumaris (begun summer 1295): in 1296 James of St George had 400 masons, 2,000 lesser workmen, 100 carts, 60 wagons, 30 boats, 200 quarrymen and 30 smiths; ≈1,800 men cost ≈£270 a week in 1295; £11,000 was spent by 1300 and £15,000 by 1330, and the castle was never finished; walls 36 ft high and 15.5 ft thick [163]. His letter of 27 February 1296 asked for £250 a week, reported walls 28 ft high in places (20 ft at the lowest), ten outer and four inner towers begun and four gates hung [164]. Vale Royal (1278–80) employed 15 quarrymen and 31 carters on average and cost over £1,500; Eton used over a million bricks in 1443–44; Henry Yevele earned 2s a day [161]. Vale Royal's quarry sent 35,448 cartloads over five miles in three years — "one cartload… about every quarter of an hour of the working day"; Westminster Abbey's weekly workforce in 1253 peaked at 428; carting stone ten miles cost as much as buying it [162]. Girona cathedral in the 15th century averaged ≈10 men a year, 30 on the first two bays, a maximum of 41 (1419), and absorbed ≈64 % of the city's 72 building workers; the ideal mix was ≈40 % skilled, 40 % unskilled, 20 % flexible [165]. Ávila's wall (begun 1090, largely rebuilt in the 12th century) runs 2,516 m with 88 towers, 9 gates, 3 m thick and 12 m high, enclosing 31 ha [166].
- **Output.** A medieval tenant could gather ≈250 kg of fallen branches a day (≈1,000 kWh); a modern axe-and-saw worker 0.5–2.5 t of firewood in eight hours; a cord weighs 1.4 t; a household burns 40–60 kWh of heat a day in winter [167]. **Not found:** a documented medieval figure for cubic feet of ashlar dressed per mason-day or tons quarried per quarryman-day; the Vale Royal cart rate (≈1 ton per 15 min for 15 quarrymen ≈ 2.5–3 t per quarryman-day) is the best proxy.
- **Wages and prices.** Labourer £2 a year c.1300 (≈2d a day); master mason 4d a day (1351), master carpenter 3d; thatchers 2d (1261–70) to 5.25d (1511–20); cow 9s 5d, sheep 1s 5d, pig 2–3s, two chickens 1d, ale 1.5d a gallon, wine 4d, an iron-bound cart 4s, a one-bay cottage £2, a craftsman's house £10–15, a riding horse £10, barons £200–500 a year [168]; Harlech Castle cost £8,190 [169]. The Assize of Bread fixed the farthing loaf at 6 lb 16s (≈2.5 kg) when wheat was 12d a quarter, shrinking with every 6d rise to ≈120 g at 20s, and ale at two gallons a penny in towns, three outside [170]; at 6s a quarter the farthing loaf weighed ≈15 oz [171].
- **Stockpiles.** Florence's cathedral works (*operai* recorded February 1296, Arte della Lana in sole control from 1331) ran the dome under Brunelleschi from 1421 to 1436 [172]; Venice kept a state grain depot (later millet) in the 13th-century Fondaco del Megio until 1797 [173]; Florence's Loggia del Grano (1619) sold grain below and stored it above [174].

### Design implications for CIVITAS (§9)

- **Calendar.** Work from sunrise to sunset: ticks 0–13,000 in summer (≈10.8 game-hours) and a winter day shortened to ticks 1,000–11,000; **45 feast days + 52 Sundays** per 365-day year → 268 working days; harvest fortnight pulls all labour to fields. Night work is forbidden except for gong farmers (21:00–05:00) and the watch.
- **Crew sizes and ratios.** 40 % skilled / 40 % unskilled / 20 % flexible; one labourer per two craftsmen on finishing work; a parish church runs 10–30 workers for 20–40 years; a town hall 30–40 for 5–10; a castle or wall circuit 400 masons + 2,000 labourers + 200 quarrymen + 160 carts/wagons at 250 units a week for 5–35 years (Beaumaris/Florence). Wall rate (COMPUTED): Florence 8,500 m in 48 years ≈ 180 m/year; Ávila 2,516 m in ≈10 years ≈ 250 m/year → **200 blocks of wall per year** for a full crew.
- **Output per shift.** Quarryman ≈2.5 t ≈ **1 stone block per shift**; mason dresses 1 ashlar block per shift (proxy, unsourced); Vale Royal's 31 carters delivering one load per quarter hour over 5 miles ≈ 4 trips per cart-day ≈ **4 t per cart-day over 8 km**; woodcutter 250 kg gathered or 0.5–2.5 t cut ≈ 1–4 log blocks per shift; a household burns ≈1 log block per 10 winter days (40–60 kWh/day ÷ 4,000 kWh/t).
- **Price ratios (labourer day-wage = 2d = 1).** Day's bread ¼; gallon of ale ¾; two chickens ½; sheep 8.5; pig 12–18; cow 56; cart 24; cottage 240; craftsman's house 1,200–1,800; master mason = 2 labourers; stone carted 10 miles = 2× quarry price.
- **Stockpiles.** Every works site keeps a lodge and yard with **≥ 90 days of stone and timber**; the granary holds a year of grain for the walled population from the market-charter tier; the arsenal tier adds the reserve forest (§6).
